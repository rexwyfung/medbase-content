# Headache / Migraine

## General

**Examiner's Pearl**: Migraine diagnostic criteria (ICHD-3): ≥5 attacks; 4-72h; ≥2 of: unilateral, pulsating, moderate-severe, aggravated by activity; ≥1 of: nausea/vomiting or photophobia+phonophobia; NOT secondary headache
**Answer**: 偏頭痛診斷標準（ICHD-3）：≥5次發作；4-72小時；≥2項：單側、搏動性、中度至重度、體力活動加重；≥1項：惡心/嘔吐或畏光+畏聲；非繼發性頭痛

**Examiner's Pearl**: SNNOOP10 red flags: Systemic symptoms (fever/weight loss), Neurological deficits, New onset >50y, Onset sudden/thunderclap, Older, Positional, Precipitated by Valsalva, Progressive, Papilloedema, Previous headache change
**Answer**: SNNOOP10紅旗症狀：全身症狀（發燒/體重減輕）、神經系統缺損、50歲後新發、突然/雷鳴樣發作、年齡較大、體位性、Valsalva誘發、進展性、視乳頭水腫、既往頭痛改變

**Examiner's Pearl**: Acute migraine treatment: Triptan (sumatriptan 50-100mg PO or 6mg SC) ± NSAID ± antiemetic (metoclopramide 10mg); rest in dark quiet room
**Answer**: 急性偏頭痛治療：曲普坦（舒馬普坦50-100mg口服或6mg皮下）±非甾體抗炎藥±止吐藥（甲氧氯普胺10mg）；在黑暗安靜的房間休息

**Examiner's Pearl**: Prophylaxis (≥4 attacks/month OR disability): Propranolol 40-80mg BD-TDS, Topiramate 25-100mg BD, Amitriptyline 10-75mg nocte, Candesartan 16mg OD
**Answer**: 預防性治療（每月≥4次或失能）：普萘洛爾40-80mg每日兩次至三次，托吡酯25-100mg每日兩次，阿米替林10-75mg每晚，坎地沙坦16mg每日一次

**Examiner's Pearl**: Medication overuse headache (MOH): using acute medication ≥10-15 days/month → paradoxical worsening; treat by withdrawal (gradual or supervised)
**Answer**: 藥物過度使用頭痛（MOH）：每月使用急性藥物≥10-15天→矛盾性惡化；通過撤藥治療（逐漸或監督下）

**Examiner's Pearl**: Thunderclap headache: worst headache of life, sudden onset, peaks in <1 min → SUBARACHNOID HAEMORRHAGE until proven otherwise → CT head immediately + LP if CT negative
**Answer**: 雷鳴樣頭痛：有生以來最嚴重的頭痛，突然發作，<1分鐘達到峰值→除非證明否則為蛛網膜下腔出血→立即CT頭部+若CT陰性則腰椎穿刺

## Station 1 — Management Interview

**Scenario**: Ms Chan, 25F, office worker. Presents with severe, throbbing, one-sided headaches lasting 24-48 hours, occurring twice a month. Accompanied by nausea and sensitivity to light. She has to take sick leave during attacks. Paracetamol does not help.

### Section 1 — Assessment (20%): 4P + BATHE + ICE
4P FRAMEWORK:
- Predisposing: Female gender, young adult, possible family history.
- Precipitating: Stress, lack of sleep, certain foods (cheese, chocolate), menstrual cycle.
- Perpetuating: Ineffective acute treatment (Paracetamol only), stress from missing work.
- Protective: No red flags (no sudden thunderclap onset, no neurological deficits, no fever).

BATHE (EXACT SCRIPTS):
- Background: "You mentioned these severe headaches happen twice a month and force you to take sick leave. How is this impacting your job and social life?"
- Affect: "How do you feel when you sense one of these headaches coming on?"
- Trouble: "What is the most debilitating part of the attack? Is it the pain, the nausea, or the sensitivity to light?"
- Handling: "Since Paracetamol isn't working, what else do you do to cope when an attack hits?"
- Empathy: "It sounds incredibly miserable to lose two days a month to such severe pain. I completely understand why you are seeking better help."

ICE (EXACT SCRIPTS):
- Ideas: "What do you think is triggering these severe headaches?"
- Concerns: "Are you worried that this might be something dangerous, like a brain tumor?"
- Expectations: "What were you hoping we could do today to help you manage these attacks better?"

### Section 2 — Problem Identification (10%)
PHYSICAL:
- Migraine without aura (classic presentation: unilateral, throbbing, nausea, photophobia).
- Must explicitly rule out secondary causes (SAH, meningitis, space-occupying lesion).

PSYCHOLOGICAL:
- Anticipatory anxiety about when the next attack will happen.

SOCIAL:
- Occupational impact: Absenteeism (2 days/month).

BARRIERS:
- Reliance on ineffective over-the-counter medication.
- Lack of trigger identification.

### Section 3 — RAPRIOP Management (50%)
REASSURE:
"I have examined your nervous system and it is completely normal. Your symptoms are classic for Migraines. While they are very painful, they are not dangerous and there is no sign of a brain tumor."

ADVICE (EXACT SCRIPTS):
- "A migraine is not just a headache; it is a sensitive brain state where blood vessels and nerves overreact to triggers."
- "I want you to keep a 'Headache Diary'. Write down what you ate, how much you slept, and where you are in your menstrual cycle when a headache hits. This helps us find your specific triggers."
- "When an attack starts, do not wait. Take the medication immediately. Retreat to a dark, quiet room and try to sleep."

PRESCRIBE:
- Acute Abortive Therapy: "Since Paracetamol doesn't work, I will prescribe a Triptan (e.g., Sumatriptan). This specifically targets the blood vessels in the brain to stop the migraine. Take it at the very first sign of the headache."
- Anti-emetic: "I will also give you medication (e.g., Metoclopramide) to stop the nausea and help your stomach absorb the painkiller."
- (Note: Prophylaxis like Propranolol or Amitriptyline is usually considered if attacks are >3-4 per month, so not strictly necessary yet, but can be discussed).

REFER:
- Not required currently.

INVESTIGATE:
- No CT or MRI needed as the history is classic and neurological exam is normal.

OBSERVE:
- Monitor frequency and severity of attacks using the diary.

PLAN:
- Follow up in 1 month to review the headache diary and see if the Triptan is working.

SAFETY NET:
"If you ever get a headache that hits you like a thunderclap in seconds, if you develop weakness on one side of your body, or if you get a high fever with a stiff neck, go to the A&E immediately."

### Section 4 — CIPS Communication Skills (20%)
CHUNK AND CHECK:
Explain how the Triptan works differently from Paracetamol, then ask, "Does it make sense why we are switching you to this specific migraine medication?"

ICE:
Directly address her fear of a brain tumor by explaining how her symptoms perfectly match a benign migraine pattern.

PATIENT-CENTRED:
"Are you comfortable keeping a diary for the next month so we can figure out exactly what is triggering these attacks?"

SIGNPOSTING:
"First, let's talk about why Paracetamol isn't working, and then we will discuss a stronger medication specifically designed for migraines."

## Station 3 — FM Primary Care Consultation

**Scenario**: Patient presents with severe, throbbing headaches on one side of the head, accompanied by nausea and sensitivity to light. Paracetamol is not helping.

### Find Reason (×0.5)
OPEN QUESTION:
"You mentioned having severe headaches that Paracetamol isn't helping. Can you tell me more about what these headaches feel like?"

ACTIVE LISTENING:
Allow the patient to describe the headache characteristics without interrupting.

### Define Problem + Systems Review (×2.5)
SOCRATES FOR HEADACHE:
- Site: "Is it on one side of your head or both?"
- Character: "Is it a throbbing, pounding pain, or a tight band?"
- Time: "How long does an attack usually last?"
- Exacerbating: "Does light, sound, or physical activity make it worse?"
- Associated: "Do you feel sick to your stomach or vomit?"
- Aura: "Do you see flashing lights or zig-zag lines before the headache starts?"

SCREEN FOR RED FLAGS (MUST ASK):
- "Have you ever had a headache that hit you suddenly like a thunderclap?" (SAH)
- "Do you have any weakness, numbness, or difficulty speaking during the headache?" (Stroke/TIA)
- "Have you had a fever or a stiff neck?" (Meningitis)
- "Is the headache worse in the morning or when you bend over?" (Raised ICP)

PHYSICAL EXAM:
- Perform a basic neurological exam (cranial nerves, power, coordination).
- Check optic fundi for papilloedema (if equipped).

### ICE (Ideas, Concerns, Expectations) (×0.5)
IDEAS:
"What do you think triggers these headaches?"

CONCERNS:
"Are you worried about a brain tumor or something serious?"

EXPECTATIONS:
"What were you hoping I could prescribe for you today?"

### Diagnosis & Explanation (×0.5)
DIAGNOSIS:
"Based on your symptoms—the throbbing pain on one side, the nausea, and sensitivity to light—you are suffering from Migraines."

EXPLANATION:
"A migraine is a neurological condition where the blood vessels and nerves in your brain become highly sensitive and inflamed. That is why normal painkillers like Paracetamol often don't work well enough."

CHECK UNDERSTANDING:
"Does that explanation make sense to you?"

### Management + RICE (×1.0)
REASSURE:
"Your neurological exam is completely normal, so there are no signs of anything dangerous like a tumor."

INVESTIGATE:
"We do not need to do a brain scan today because your symptoms are classic for migraines and your exam is normal."

PLAN:
"I will prescribe a specific migraine medication called a Triptan. You must take it at the very first sign of the headache. I will also give you an anti-sickness tablet."

ADVICE:
"Keep a headache diary to track what you eat, your sleep, and stress levels to identify your triggers. When an attack hits, rest in a dark, quiet room."

SAFETY NET:
"If you ever get a sudden thunderclap headache, develop weakness on one side, or have a stiff neck with a fever, go to the A&E immediately."

## Station 4 — Medical Record + Prescription

### Subjective (S)
Miss Yuen Hoi Ling (F/30), secondary school teacher, 2-year history of recurrent headaches — approx. 6 attacks/month, each lasting 12-24 hours. Character: right-sided, throbbing, moderate-severe (7/10), associated nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia. Worsened by movement; relieved by dark quiet room and sleep. NO aura. Prodrome: yawning + food craving (reported in retrospect). Currently taking Ibuprofen 400mg 1-2 tabs almost daily (estimated 20 days/month). This has been occurring ×3 months. No menstrual correlation identified. PMH: Nil. NKDA. Social: Non-smoker. Occasional wine (weekends). Regular coffee (2 cups/day). Sleep: irregular (marking periods + early morning teaching). FH: Mother has migraine. Worried about brain tumour (stated explicitly).

### Objective (O)
Vitals: BP 112/72 mmHg, HR 74 bpm, afebrile.
General: Alert, comfortable at time of consultation (not in acute attack).
Neurological:
  Cranial nerves: CN II-XII all intact (visual acuity 6/6 bilaterally; visual fields full to confrontation).
  Fundoscopy: Discs sharp bilaterally (no papilloedema).
  Motor: Power 5/5 all limbs; reflexes 2+ symmetric; no pronator drift.
  Sensory: Intact to light touch and vibration.
  Cerebellar: No dysmetria, no dysdiadochokinesia; Romberg negative.
  Gait: Normal.
Neck: No meningism. Cranial percussion non-tender.
Temporal arteries: Non-tender, pulsatile (temporal arteritis excluded — appropriate in >50y; not the concern here).

### Assessment (A)
PRIMARY DIAGNOSIS: 
1. Migraine without aura (ICHD-3 criteria fully met: ≥5 attacks; 12-24h duration; unilateral+pulsating+severe+movement aggravation; nausea+photophobia+phonophobia; normal neurology)
2. Probable Medication Overuse Headache (MOH) — ibuprofen use >15 days/month × 3 months

NOT: Intracranial tumour (normal neurological examination, no papilloedema, no red flags, no progressive pattern); tension-type headache (pulsating, severe, nausea/photophobia, activity aggravation = migraine not TTH); subarachnoid haemorrhage (not thunderclap onset, not "worst headache of life").

RISK FACTORS FOR MOH: Oestrogen combined OCP (does not apply — not on OCP), daily analgesic use (confirmed).

### Plan (P)
1. STOP IBUPROFEN (MOH treatment): Withdraw ibuprofen gradually over 2-4 weeks. Warn: headache may temporarily worsen for 4-6 weeks during withdrawal — this is expected and not a sign of deterioration. Bridge with paracetamol 1g PRN if needed during withdrawal (paracetamol has lower MOH risk than NSAIDs/triptans).

2. ACUTE MIGRAINE TREATMENT (replace ibuprofen):
   a) Sumatriptan 50mg oral tablet — take at ONSET of headache (not during aura; not waiting until severe). Maximum 2 doses in 24h (100mg total oral). Do NOT use >10 days/month (will cause MOH).
   b) Metoclopramide 10mg oral — take simultaneously with sumatriptan (reduces nausea, enhances sumatriptan absorption).
   c) Rest in dark, quiet room.

3. PROPHYLAXIS (indicated — ≥4 attacks/month, significant disability):
   Propranolol 40mg twice daily. Increase to 80mg twice daily after 4 weeks if tolerated and insufficient response.
   Counsel: Takes 4-8 weeks for full effect. Do not stop abruptly. Monitor HR and BP. Avoid in asthma.

4. TRIGGER MANAGEMENT: Headache diary (track: date, severity, duration, triggers, medications used). Aim for regular sleep, regular meals, limit caffeine (max 1 cup/day; avoid cold-turkey withdrawal). Manage work stress.

5. INVESTIGATIONS: CT Head NOT indicated (normal neurology, typical history, no red flags, age <50 — imaging has very low yield and risks unnecessary radiation). If atypical features develop → reconsider.

6. SAFETY NET: "If you have the worst headache of your life — sudden, severe, within seconds — go to A&E immediately (possible SAH). If any headache is accompanied by fever, stiff neck, or rash — also A&E. If you develop weakness, speech problems, or vision loss — call 999."

7. FOLLOW-UP: 4-6 weeks. Review headache diary, sumatriptan response, propranolol tolerance. Adjust prophylaxis dose at 3 months.

### Prescription
- **Drug**: Sumatriptan succinate
- **Preparation**: Tablets
- **Dose**: 50mg
- **Route**: Oral
- **Frequency**: At onset of migraine attack (PRN)
- **Duration**: PRN (use maximum 10 days/month)
- **Quantity**: 12 tablets

**Notes**: Take 1 tablet at FIRST sign of migraine headache. Do NOT take during aura — wait for headache to begin. If headache returns after 2h, may take one further tablet (max 2 tablets = 100mg in 24h). DO NOT use more than 10 days/month (risk of medication overuse headache). CONTRAINDICATED in ischaemic heart disease, uncontrolled hypertension, stroke, Raynaud's disease, peripheral vascular disease.

**Tips**:
- "Sumatriptan succinate" (generic) NOT "Imigran" alone
- "50mg" — most patients start with 50mg; some need 100mg
- "Oral" route — state it (available as SC, nasal spray, wafer)
- "PRN" (as required) — state the indication: "at onset of migraine"
- "12 tablets" — prescribe limited quantity (monitor for overuse)
- CONTRAINDICATIONS: Cardiovascular disease, uncontrolled HTN — CHECK BEFORE PRESCRIBING
- SEROTONIN SYNDROME risk if combined with SSRIs/SNRIs — rare but important
- Pregnancy: Category C — avoid in pregnancy; use paracetamol instead
