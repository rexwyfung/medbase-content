# Medial and Lateral Epicondylitis (內外上髁炎 / 高爾夫球肘與網球肘)

## 1. Clinical Definition, Mechanism of Injury (MOI) & Epidemiology

### Definition of Epicondylitis
* **Pathology**: Not true inflammation (-itis); rather angiofibroblastic tendinosis (microtearing, failed healing, neovascularization) at common extensor/flexor origins.
* **Terminology**: 
  * **Lateral Epicondylitis (網球肘)**: Affects **Common Extensor Origin (CEO)**.
  * **Medial Epicondylitis (高爾夫球肘)**: Affects **Common Flexor Origin (CFO)**.

### Lateral Epicondylitis (Tennis Elbow) Profile
* **MOI**: Repetitive microtrauma from eccentric overload during wrist extension & forearm supination (e.g., backhand stroke, screwdriver use).
* **Epidemiology**: Most common overuse elbow injury (7-10x > Medial).
* **Demographics**: 35-50 years old, M=F, dominant arm > non-dominant arm.

### Medial Epicondylitis (Golfer's Elbow) Profile
* **MOI**: Repetitive microtrauma from resisted wrist flexion & forearm pronation (e.g., golf swing, baseball pitching).
* **Epidemiology**: Less common (10-20% of all epicondylitis).
* **Demographics**: 35-50 years old, often affects throwing athletes, golfers, manual laborers.

## 2. Applied Orthopaedic Anatomy & Biomechanics

### Lateral Elbow Anatomy & Biomechanics
* **Primary Culprit**: **Extensor Carpi Radialis Brevis (ECRB) (橈側腕短伸肌)**.
* **Secondary Muscles**: Extensor digitorum communis (EDC), Extensor carpi radialis longus (ECRL).
* **Biomechanics**: ECRB abuts against the **Capitellum (肱骨小頭)** during elbow extension & pronation → mechanical friction & avascularity at origin.
* **Vascular Supply**: "Hypovascular zone" 2-3cm distal to CEO origin → predisposes to failed healing.

### Medial Elbow Anatomy & Biomechanics
* **Primary Culprits**: **Pronator Teres (旋前圓肌)** & **Flexor Carpi Radialis (FCR) (橈側腕屈肌)**.
* **Secondary Muscles**: Flexor carpi ulnaris (FCU), Palmaris longus (PL).
* **Biomechanics**: Dynamic valgus stabilizers of the medial elbow during the late cocking/acceleration phase of throwing.

### Pathophysiology (Angiofibroblastic Tendinosis)
* **Histology (Nirschl)**: Disorganized, immature collagen (Type III > Type I).
* **Cellular**: Fibroblastic hyperplasia, absence of acute inflammatory cells (macrophages/neutrophils).
* **Vascular**: Disorganized neovascularization (angiogenesis).

## 3. Clinical Assessment

### Lateral Epicondylitis Physical Signs
* **Look**: Usually normal; occasionally mild swelling at lateral elbow.
* **Feel**: Point tenderness 1-2cm distal to the **Lateral Epicondyle (肱骨外上髁)** (at ECRB origin).
* **Move**: Pain exacerbated by active wrist extension or passive wrist flexion with elbow extended.

### Medial Epicondylitis Physical Signs
* **Look**: Normal; rule out valgus deformity.
* **Feel**: Point tenderness 5-10mm distal and anterior to the **Medial Epicondyle (肱骨內上髁)**.
* **Move**: Pain exacerbated by active wrist flexion or passive wrist extension with elbow extended.

### Provocative Tests for Lateral Epicondylitis
| Provocative Test | Technique | Positive Finding |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cozen's Test** | Resisted wrist extension with forearm pronated & elbow flexed | Pain at CEO |
| **Mill's Test** | Passive maximal wrist flexion with forearm pronated & elbow extended | Pain at CEO |
| **Maudsley's Test** | Resisted extension of the long finger (middle finger) | Pain at CEO (stresses EDC) |

### Provocative Tests for Medial Epicondylitis
| Provocative Test | Technique | Positive Finding |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Golfer's Elbow Test** | Resisted wrist flexion & forearm pronation with elbow extended | Pain at CFO |

### Neurovascular (NV) Assessment
* **Lateral Side (PIN)**: Assess **Posterior Interosseous Nerve (PIN)** (finger extension). R/O Radial Tunnel Syndrome (tenderness 4cm distal to epicondyle over mobile wad).
* **Medial Side (Ulnar Nerve)**: Assess **Ulnar Nerve (尺神經)** (Tinel's sign at cubital tunnel, intrinsic hand muscle strength). Co-exists in 20% of medial epicondylitis.

## 4. Orthopaedic Classification Systems

### Nirschl Pathological Stages of Tendinosis
| Stage | Histological Description | Clinical Correlation |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Stage 1** | Inflammatory, no pathological alteration | Reversible acute pain |
| **Stage 2** | Angiofibroblastic degeneration (tendinosis) | Chronic symptomatic pain |
| **Stage 3** | Structural failure / Micro-rupture | Weakness, refractory pain |
| **Stage 4** | Fibrosis and calcification | End-stage, irreversible changes |

## 5. Radiological Investigations

### X-Ray Findings
* **Standard Views**: AP & Lateral of elbow.
* **Findings**: Usually entirely normal.
* **Rule Out**: Osteoarthritis (OA), Osteochondritis Dissecans (OCD) of capitellum, loose bodies.
* **20% Cases**: May show amorphous calcifications adjacent to epicondyles (Nirschl Stage 4).

### Ultrasound (USG) Findings
* **Utility**: First-line imaging for tendinopathy evaluation in HK clinics.
* **Findings**: Tendon thickening, hypoechoic regions (focal degeneration), micro-tears, cortical irregularity.
* **Doppler**: +ve for neovascularization.

### MRI Findings
* **Utility**: Gold standard for refractory cases prior to surgery; r/o collateral ligament tears (UCL/LCL).
* **T1**: Thickened CEO/CFO tendon.
* **T2**: ↑ Intrasubstance signal intensity indicating edema, degeneration, or partial/full-thickness tears.

## 6. Initial ED/Acute Management

### Acute Flare Management Protocol
* **Rest**: Immediate cessation of inciting activities (sports, repetitive occupational tasks).
* **Ice**: Cryotherapy (15 mins q4-6h) to ↓ metabolic demand and acute pain.
* **Pharmacology**: Short course Oral NSAIDs / topical NSAID gels (symptomatic relief only, does not alter disease course).

### Orthotic Splinting
* **Counterforce Brace (網球肘帶)**: Applied 2-3cm distal to epicondyle.
* **Mechanism**: Compresses muscle belly → disperses mechanical forces away from the damaged enthesis during contraction.
* **Wrist Splint**: Neutral wrist splint at night to relax ECRB/flexors (acute severe phase only).

## 7. Definitive Management Algorithm

### Management Algorithm & Indications
| Management Phase | Indications | Principle |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Non-Operative (First-Line)** | All new diagnoses; initial 6-12 months | Stimulate biological healing, offload tendon |
| **Operative (Surgical)** | Failed conservative >6-12 months, intractable pain, severe ADL limitation | Excision of pathological tissue, stimulate bleeding |

### Non-Operative Modalities
* **Physiotherapy (Primary stay)**: **Eccentric loading exercises** (gold standard to stimulate collagen realignment).
* **Extracorporeal Shockwave Therapy (ESWT) (衝擊波治療)**: Microtrauma induces neovascularization and tissue regeneration (highly utilized in HK HA/private).
* **Corticosteroid Injection (CSI)**: Provides excellent *short-term* (<6 weeks) relief but ↑ risk of long-term recurrence & tendon atrophy. Use sparingly.
* **Biologics (PRP/Autologous Blood)**: Delivers growth factors to hypovascular zone (increasingly used in HK private sector).

### Operative Techniques
* **Lateral - Nirschl Procedure (Open / Arthroscopic)**: 
  * Identify CEO, split EDC longitudinally.
  * Excise angiofibroblastic tissue at ECRB origin.
  * Decorticate / drill lateral epicondyle to stimulate bleeding (marrow elements).
  * Reattach healthy tendon to bone.
* **Medial - CFO Debridement**:
  * Excise pathological tissue at FCR/Pronator teres origin.
  * **Ulnar Nerve**: Concomitant cubital tunnel decompression / anterior transposition if pre-operative ulnar neuropathy +ve.

## 8. Rehabilitation Protocol

### Phase 1: Acute/Inflammatory (Weeks 0-2)
* **Goal**: Pain control.
* **Actions**: Counterforce brace, avoid provocative gripping/lifting, gentle pain-free isometric contractions.

### Phase 2: Restorative/Strengthening (Weeks 3-8)
* **Goal**: Tendon remodeling.
* **Actions**: **Eccentric strengthening** (e.g., lowering a light weight slowly with the wrist).
* **Progression**: Isometrics → Concentric → Eccentric loading.

### Phase 3: Return to Play/Work (Weeks 8-12+)
* **Goal**: Functional restoration.
* **Actions**: Sport-specific mechanics correction (e.g., racquet grip sizing, altering backhand technique).
* **Equipment**: Use racquets with lower string tension and larger grips to ↓ torque.

## 9. Orthopaedic Complications

### Disease-Related Complications
* **Chronic Recalcitrant Pain**: Progression to Nirschl Stage 4.
* **Tendon Rupture**: CEO/CFO rupture (rare naturally, mostly post-CSI).
* **Secondary Stiffness**: Elbow contracture secondary to guarding/disuse.

### Iatrogenic/Treatment Complications
* **From Corticosteroid Injection**: Subcutaneous fat atrophy, skin depigmentation, iatrogenic tendon rupture, "steroid flare" (acute post-injection pain).
* **From Lateral Surgery**: **PIN injury** (if dissection strays >1cm distal to radiocapitellar joint), LCL iatrogenic injury (creating posterolateral rotatory instability - PLRI).
* **From Medial Surgery**: **Ulnar nerve injury**, Medial Ulnar Collateral Ligament (MUCL) iatrogenic injury (valgus instability).

## 10. Examiner's Pearls

* Viva Question: What is the main pathophysiological process in tennis elbow? Answer: Angiofibroblastic tendinosis, not acute inflammation.
* Viva Question: Which specific tendon is most commonly involved in lateral epicondylitis? Answer: Extensor Carpi Radialis Brevis (ECRB).
* Trap: Treating epicondylitis strictly with anti-inflammatory medications long-term. Pearl: NSAIDs only treat symptoms; eccentric physiotherapy is required for collagen remodeling and definitive healing.
* Pitfall: Performing a corticosteroid injection directly into the tendon substance. This causes collagen necrosis and increases the risk of complete tendon rupture.
* Viva Question: A patient with lateral elbow pain also complains of weakness in extending their fingers, but wrist extension is intact. What is the diagnosis? Answer: Radial Tunnel Syndrome (PIN compression), which can mimic or co-exist with lateral epicondylitis.
* Trap: Releasing the entire extensor origin during lateral epicondylitis surgery. Pearl: Excessive release risks severing the Lateral Ulnar Collateral Ligament (LUCL), leading to iatrogenic posterolateral rotatory instability (PLRI).
* Pitfall: Failing to assess the ulnar nerve in a patient with medial epicondylitis. Up to 20% of patients have concomitant cubital tunnel syndrome requiring surgical transposition if non-operative management fails.
