# Peripheral Nerve Injuries (周圍神經損傷)

## 1. Clinical Definition, Mechanism of Injury (MOI) & Epidemiology

### Definition of Peripheral Nerve Injury
* **Definition**: Structural disruption or functional impairment of peripheral nerves (周圍神經), ranging from local ischemia to complete axonal transection.
* **Pathophysiology**: Neural tissue ischemia → axonal degradation → Wallerian degeneration distal to site of injury if axon is interrupted.

### Mechanisms of Injury (MOI)
* **Traction / Stretch**: High-velocity traction (e.g., brachial plexus traction during birth or motorcycle accidents).
* **Laceration / Transection**: Sharp trauma (e.g., glass cuts, stab wounds, iatrogenic surgical laceration).
* **Compression / Entrapment**: Chronic entrapment (e.g., carpal tunnel, cubital tunnel) or acute compression (e.g., tourniquet palsy, Saturday night palsy).
* **Thermal / Electrical**: High-voltage electrical burns or thermal injury.
* **Ischemia**: Compartment syndrome or prolonged arterial occlusion leading to ischemic neuritis.

### Epidemiology & Risk Factors
* **Demographics**: Frequently affects young adults following high-energy trauma (motor vehicle accidents, industrial injuries) or older adults with degenerative entrapment neuropathies.
* **Anatomical Vulnerability**: Nerves traversing tight osseofascial tunnels (e.g., median nerve at carpal tunnel, ulnar nerve at cubital groove, radial nerve at spiral groove) are at highest risk of compression and traction injuries.


## 2. Applied Orthopaedic Anatomy & Biomechanics (Vascular supply, deforming muscle forces)

### Neural Micro-Anatomy & Vascular Supply
* **Epineurium**: Outer protective dense connective tissue sheath containing longitudinal blood vessels (extrinsic vascular supply).
* **Perineurium**: Intermediate sheath providing tensile strength and forming the blood-nerve barrier.
* **Endoneurium**:
	* Delicate connective tissue surrounding individual nerve fibers (Schwann cells and axons).
	* Contains rich interconnected capillary network (intrinsic vascular supply).
* **Vulnerability to Ischemia**:
	* Peripheral nerves possess high metabolic demands.
	* Compression >30 mmHg impairs epineurial blood flow, leading to intraneural edema and hypoxia.
![image|500](https://pub-f0606dedf6d94e4bb5b1999cbd065914.r2.dev/paediatrics/c7778c018ed1d986734d112ce2fbba0a.png)

### Biomechanics & Viscoelastic Properties
* **Tensile Strength**:
	* Perineurium provides primary tensile strength.
	* Nerve elongation up to 6% is well-tolerated without ischemia.
* **Elongation Limits**: Elongation exceeding 15% causes complete microvascular occlusion and irreversible axonal damage.
* **Deforming Forces in Upper Limb Lesions**:
  * **Radial Nerve (Spiral Groove)**: Loss of extensors → unopposed flexors cause **wrist drop (腕下垂)**.
  * **Median Nerve (High/Low)**: Loss of thenar function and lateral lumbricals → unopposed long flexors and extensors lead to **ape hand (猿手)**.
  * **Ulnar Nerve (Guyon's/Cubital)**: Loss of interossei and medial lumbricals → unopposed extensors at MCPJs and flexors at IPJs lead to **claw hand (爪形手)**.

## 3. Clinical Assessment

### Look (Inspection)
* **Skin & Trophic Changes**: Assess for skin dryness, smooth shiny skin, loss of hair, nail dystrophy, and ulceration in autonomous sensory zones.
* **Postural Deformities**: Identify specific static deformities (e.g., wrist drop, ape hand, claw hand, "waiter's tip" posture).
* **Muscle Atrophy**: Inspect for wasting of specific muscle groups:
  * Thenar eminence (Median nerve).
  * Hypothenar eminence and first dorsal interosseous (Ulnar nerve).
  * Forearm muscle bulk (Radial/Median/Ulnar lesions).

### Feel (Palpation)
* **Tender Points**: Palpate along nerve courses for Tinel's sign (tapping over nerve elicits distal tingling).
* **Neuroma Tenderness**: Palpate tender, localized painful nodules at sites of prior trauma or transection.
* **Soft Tissues**: Assess local scar tissue mobility and surrounding compartment tensions.

### Move (Active & Passive Range of Motion)
* **Manual Muscle Testing (MMT)**: Graded 0/5 to 5/5 for specific key motor groups:
  * **C5**: Deltoid (Shoulder abduction).
  * **C6**: Biceps brachii / Extensor carpi radialis longus (Elbow flexion / Wrist extension).
  * **C7**: Triceps brachii / Flexor carpi radialis (Elbow extension / Wrist flexion).
  * **C8**: Flexor digitorum profundus (Finger flexion).
  * **T1**: First dorsal interosseous / Abductor digiti minimi (Finger abduction/adduction).
  * **Median Nerve**: Thumb opposition (Abductor pollicis brevis), wrist flexion.
  * **Ulnar Nerve**: Finger crossing, Froment's sign (adductor pollicis compensation).
  * **Radial Nerve**: Wrist extension, thumb extension (Posterior interosseous nerve - PIN).
![image|200](https://pub-f0606dedf6d94e4bb5b1999cbd065914.r2.dev/paediatrics/4517187915027e0850d2bcd14834dbf7.png)

### Neurovascular (NV) Status
* **Autonomous Sensory Zones (ASZ)**:
  * **Radial Nerve**: Dorsal first web space.
  * **Median Nerve**: Volar pad of index finger.
  * **Ulnar Nerve**: Volar pad of little finger.
* **Two-Point Discrimination (2PD)**: Normal static 2PD <6 mm.
* **Vascular Check**: Palpate distal pulses (radial, ulnar) and capillary refill time to exclude concomitant vascular trauma.

## 4. Orthopaedic Classification Systems (Eponyms & Grading)

### Seddon Classification
| Grade / Type    | Pathological Description                                                  | Prognosis & Recovery                                       |
| :-------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------ | :--------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Neuropraxia** | Localized myelin ischemia/damage; axon and sheath intact.                 | Complete spontaneous recovery within days to weeks.        |
| **Axonotmesis** | Axon disrupted; endoneurial sheath intact. Wallerian degeneration occurs. | Good recovery via axonal regeneration (~1 mm/day).         |
| **Neurotmesis** | Complete anatomical transection of nerve and supporting sheaths.          | No recovery without surgical repair; high risk of neuroma. |

![image|400](https://pub-f0606dedf6d94e4bb5b1999cbd065914.r2.dev/paediatrics/ce8a9ede970b1e2da92de44ae8b7b23e.png)

### Sunderland Classification
| Grade / Type | Pathological Description                                         | Prognosis & Recovery                                               |
| :----------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Grade 1**  | Equivalent to Seddon Neuropraxia (conduction block).             | Excellent; rapid and complete.                                     |
| **Grade 2**  | Axon disrupted, endoneurium intact.                              | Good; spontaneous axonal regrowth.                                 |
| **Grade 3**  | Axon and endoneurium disrupted; perineurium intact.              | Variable; scarring causes misdirection, requires prolonged time.   |
| **Grade 4**  | Axon, endoneurium, and perineurium disrupted; epineurium intact. | Poor; internal scar blocks regeneration; surgical excision needed. |
| **Grade 5**  | Complete nerve trunk transection (Neurotmesis).                  | None without microsurgical intervention.                           |

![image|350](https://pub-f0606dedf6d94e4bb5b1999cbd065914.r2.dev/paediatrics/fc51498dca1644123b7c52edf74ed61a.png)

### Brachial Plexus Injury Classifications
* **Erb's Palsy (Upper Trunk - C5, C6)**:
  * **Anatomy**: Roots C5-C6 (Erb's point).
  * **Clinical Posture**: **"Waiter's tip" (侍應生小費姿勢)** (shoulder adducted, internally rotated, elbow extended, forearm pronated).
* **Klumpke's Palsy (Lower Trunk - C8, T1)**:
  * **Anatomy**: Roots C8-T1.
  * **Clinical Posture**: Intrinsic hand muscle paralysis (claw hand), sensory loss along ulnar border, possible Horner's syndrome if T1 sympathetic chain involved.

## 5. Radiological Investigations

### Plain Radiographs (X-rays)
* **Indications**: Rule out associated fractures, dislocations, or bony impingements (e.g., clavicle fracture, shoulder dislocation, humerus shaft fracture).
* **Views**:
	* Standard orthogonal views of the affected limb joints.
	* Cervical spine views for brachial plexus injuries.

### Advanced Imaging
* **High-Resolution Ultrasound (USG)**:
  * **Utility**: Non-invasive visualization of nerve continuity, neuroma formation, localized neuromas in continuity, and dynamic nerve entrapment (e.g., cubital tunnel subluxation).
* **Magnetic Resonance Neurography (MRN)**:
  * **Utility**: High-resolution specialized T2-weighted and diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) sequences to delineate nerve root avulsions, traction injuries, and intraneural pathologies.
![image|350](https://pub-f0606dedf6d94e4bb5b1999cbd065914.r2.dev/paediatrics/23d83d799de67976038f76e069db556d.png)

### Electrophysiological Studies
* **Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS)**:
  * **Findings**:
	  * Decreased compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude.
	  * Slowed conduction velocities.
	  * Prolonged latency.
	  * Differentiates demyelination (neuropraxia) from axonal loss.
* **Electromyography (EMG)**:
  * **Findings**:
	  * Fibrillation potentials and positive sharp waves indicate active denervation (2-3 weeks post-injury).
	  * Polyphasic reinnervation potentials indicate recovery.
![image|500](https://pub-f0606dedf6d94e4bb5b1999cbd065914.r2.dev/paediatrics/c013f87d81d63f1dac55f3d0593d2276.png)


## 6. Initial ED Management (Reduction, Splinting/Casting, Traction)

### Acute Resuscitation & Stabilization
* **Primary Survey (ATLS)**: Prioritize life-threatening injuries, control active hemorrhage, and stabilize skeletal trauma before focusing on isolated nerve repairs.
* **Open Wound Management**:
	* Irrigate open wounds thoroughly.
	* Remove gross contaminants.
	* Administer prompt broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics and tetanus prophylaxis.

### Splinting & Immobilization
* **Splint Application**: Apply supportive splints in safe functional positions to prevent secondary joint contractures and protect vulnerable denervated muscle units:
  * *Radial Nerve Injury*: **Wrist cock-up splint** (wrist in 30° extension, MCPJs free).
  * *Median / Ulnar Nerve Injury*: Intrinsic plus splinting (MCPJs flexed at 70°, IPJs fully extended) to prevent clawing and joint stiffness.
* **Traction Management**: Avoid aggressive, unmonitored skeletal traction that could exacerbate stretch injuries of the brachial plexus.

## 7. Definitive Management Algorithm (Non-Operative vs. Operative Indications & Techniques)
### Non-Operative Management Indications & Techniques
- **Indications:**
	- Closed injuries with incomplete deficits.
	- Seddon Grade 1 (Neuropraxia).
	- Early Axonotmesis showing signs of recovery on serial exams/EMG.
- **Specific Techniques & Principles:**
	- Observation.
	- Serial Clinical Examinations.
	- Repeated NCS/EMG at 3-6 weeks.
	- Customized splinting.
	- Aggressive passive/active-assisted range of motion therapy.


### Operative Management Indications & Techniques

| Management Strategy      | Indications                                                                                                                                    | Specific Techniques & Principles                                                                                                                              |
| :----------------------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Decompression**        | - Chronic compression neuropathies unresponsive to conservative care.<br>- Acute compartment syndrome.<br>- Progressive neurological deficits. | Open or endoscopic surgical release (e.g., Carpal Tunnel Release, Cubital Tunnel Release / In-situ decompression or anterior transposition).                  |
| **Primary Neurorrhaphy** | Sharp clean-cut lacerations (Neurotmesis) with tension-free ends.                                                                              | Microscope-assisted epineurial or perineurial repair using fine monofilament sutures (8-0 or 9-0 nylon) performed within 7-10 days.                           |
| **Nerve Grafting**       | - Gap defects preventing tension-free primary repair.<br>- Resection of severe fibrotic neuromas-in-continuity.                                | Interposition nerve grafts harvested from autologous donor sites (e.g., sural nerve, medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve).                                    |
| **Tendon Transfers**     | - Irreparable nerve injuries.<br>- Delayed presentations (>12-18 months).<br>- Failed primary nerve repairs.                                   | Transfer functional expendable tendons to restore lost motor functions (e.g., pronator teres to ECRB for radial nerve palsy; FDS to index for ulnar clawing). |


## 8. Rehabilitation Protocol

### Phase 1: Acute Protection & Edema Control (0-3 Weeks Post-Injury/Surgery)
* **Splinting Maintenance**: Strict continuous wear of custom orthoses protecting repair sites or vulnerable paralyzed joints.
* **Edema Control**: Elevation, retrograde massage, and compression garments to minimize intraneural and interstitial swelling.
* **Passive Range of Motion (PROM)**: Perform gentle passive exercises daily for all unconstrained joints to prevent joint contractures and tendon adhesions.

### Phase 2: Early Mobilization & Sensory Re-Education (3-8 Weeks)
* **Active-Assisted ROM**: Transition to active-assisted motion under therapist supervision once surgical clearance or early reinnervation signs appear.
* **Sensory Re-Education**: Implement desensitization techniques (textural grading, vibration) and localization tasks to prevent cortical sensory map degradation.
* **Electrical Stimulation**: Use functional electrical stimulation (FES) to maintain muscle fiber viability and reduce disuse atrophy.

### Phase 3: Strengthening & Functional Integration (8 Weeks to 12 Months)
* **Progressive Resistance Training**: Introduce light resistive exercises once active motor units (≥ Medical Research Council Grade 3/4) are documented.
* **Functional Task Practice**: Incorporate adaptive equipment and task-specific training for activities of daily living (ADLs).
* **Axonal Tracking**: Monitor recovery rate (~ 1 mm/day or 1 inch/month) using serial Tinel's percussion mapping.


## 9. Orthopaedic Complications

### Neurological & Sensory Complications
* **Neuroma Formation**: Painful, disorganized proliferation of axons at the proximal stump of a transected nerve, resulting in severe local tenderness and dysesthesia.
* **Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS)**: Autonomic dysfunction characterized by severe burning pain, vasomotor instability, skin changes, and joint stiffness following minor nerve trauma.
* **Permanent Deficits**: Irrecoverable sensory loss or permanent muscle paralysis due to delayed presentation, extensive zone of injury, or failed surgical reinnervation.

### Musculoskeletal Sequelae
* **Joint Contractures**: Fixed flexion or extension deformities resulting from prolonged immobilization and unopposed deforming muscle forces.
* **Severe Muscle Atrophy**: Irreversible fibrous replacement of muscle architecture following prolonged denervation exceeding 12-18 months, rendering muscle unresponsive to reinnervation.
* **Trophic Ulceration**: Chronic skin breakdown and painless burns over autonomous sensory zones due to absent protective sensation.


## 10. Examiner's Pearls

* Viva Question: What is the critical time window for performing a primary nerve repair in a sharp laceration? Answer: Within 7 to 10 days post-injury, before epineurial fibrosis sets in and retraction complicates tension-free coaptation.
* Trap: Assuming that a closed fracture with a nerve deficit requires immediate emergency open exploration. Pearl: Most closed fracture-related nerve palsies (e.g., radial nerve palsy with humeral shaft fractures) are neuropraxia or axonotmesis; observe for 3 months unless high-energy open trauma or penetrating injury is present.
* Pitfall: Confusing Seddon's neuropraxia with neurotmesis on initial presentation. Explain of how to avoid: Perform serial clinical examinations and confirmatory nerve conduction studies (NCS) at 3 to 4 weeks to verify if action potentials cross the injury site.
* Viva Question: What clinical sign distinguishes high radial nerve palsy from posterior interosseous nerve (PIN) syndrome? Answer: High radial nerve palsy presents with both wrist drop and sensory loss over the dorsal first web space, whereas PIN syndrome preserves wrist extension (ECRL intact) and has no sensory deficit.
	![image|450](https://pub-f0606dedf6d94e4bb5b1999cbd065914.r2.dev/paediatrics/795a2b11c335b2b752bdb923533367f1.png)
* Trap: Relying solely on clinical Tinel's sign to measure nerve recovery rate. Pearl: Tinel's sign indicates axonal sprouting but does not confirm functional motor or sensory reinnervation; always correlate with manual muscle testing and electrodiagnostics.
* Pitfall: Failing to protect a repaired nerve tension-free during early postoperative rehabilitation. Explain of how to avoid: Maintain rigid splint immobilization for 3 weeks post-neurorrhaphy before initiating gentle protected range-of-motion exercises.
