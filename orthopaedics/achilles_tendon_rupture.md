# Rupture of Tendo Achilles (跟腱斷裂)

## 1. Clinical Definition, Mechanism of Injury (MOI) & Epidemiology

### Clinical Definition of Tendo Achilles Rupture
* **Definition:** Complete or partial disruption of the **Achilles tendon** (conjoined tendon of gastrocnemius & soleus).
* **Nature:** Acute traumatic tear or chronic degenerative rupture.

### Mechanism of Injury (MOI) for Achilles Rupture
* **Push-off MOI:** Sudden forced plantarflexion with knee extended (e.g., sprinting, jumping in basketball/badminton).
* **Dorsiflexion MOI:** Sudden unexpected dorsiflexion of an actively plantarflexed ankle (e.g., slipping on stairs).
* **Direct Trauma MOI:** Direct blow to a taut tendon (less common).

### Epidemiology & Risk Factors of Achilles Rupture
* **Demographics:** Peak incidence 30-50 years, predominantly male (M:F = 5:1).
* **Patient Profile:** Classic "weekend warrior" (infrequent high-intensity sports).
* **Extrinsic Risk Factors:** 
  * **Fluoroquinolone** antibiotics (e.g., Ciprofloxacin).
  * Local **Corticosteroid** injections (類固醇注射).
* **Intrinsic Risk Factors:** Gout, Rheumatoid Arthritis, Systemic Lupus Erythematosus, end-stage renal failure.

## 2. Applied Orthopaedic Anatomy & Biomechanics

### Achilles Tendon Anatomy & Muscle Forces
* **Structure:** Largest & strongest tendon in the body. Conjoined tendon of **gastrocnemius** (crosses knee & ankle) and **soleus** (crosses ankle only).
* **Insertion:** Middle third of the posterior surface of the **calcaneus (跟骨)**.
* **Biomechanics:** Primary plantarflexor of the ankle. Handles loads up to 10x body weight during running.

### Vascular Supply & Watershed Zone of Achilles
* **Arterial Supply:** Branches from **posterior tibial artery** & **peroneal artery**.
* **Vascular Watershed Zone:** Located **2 to 6 cm proximal to calcaneal insertion**.
* **Significance:** Area of hypovascularity → most frequent site of rupture & poor healing potential.

### Sural Nerve (腓腸神經) Anatomy
* **Location:** Descends posterior to the lateral malleolus, running along the lateral border of the Achilles tendon.
* **Significance:** At high risk of iatrogenic injury during percutaneous/minimally invasive repair approaches.

## 3. Clinical Assessment

### Look & Feel for Achilles Rupture
* **Look:**
  * Swelling and ecchymosis around the posterior ankle.
  * Loss of normal resting equinus tension (foot rests at 90° rather than slight plantarflexion compared to contralateral leg).
* **Feel:**
  * Palpable **gap defect** (hatchet strike defect) typically 2-6 cm proximal to insertion.
  * Tenderness over the tendon.

### Move & Special Tests for Achilles Rupture
* **Move:**
  * Weak or absent active plantarflexion (pt unable to perform single-leg heel raise).
  * +ve active plantarflexion does NOT exclude rupture (FHL, FDL, Tibialis Posterior, Peroneus Longus compensate).
* **Thompson Test (Simmonds' Test):**
  * Pt prone, knee flexed to 90°. Squeeze calf muscle.
  * **+ve sign:** Lack of passive plantarflexion = complete rupture.
* **Matles Test:**
  * Pt prone, actively flex knees to 90°.
  * **+ve sign:** Affected foot falls into neutral or dorsiflexion (loss of resting equinus).
* **O'Brien Needle Test:**
  * Needle inserted 10cm proximal to calcaneus into tendon. Passively dorsiflex/plantarflex foot.
  * **+ve sign:** Hub of needle does not move (indicates distal continuity lost).

## 4. Orthopaedic Classification Systems

### Kuwada Classification of Achilles Tendon Tears
| Type | Description / Defect Size |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Type I** | Partial tear (≤ 50% tendon involvement) |
| **Type II** | Complete tear with defect gap ≤ 3 cm |
| **Type III** | Complete tear with defect gap 3 to 6 cm |
| **Type IV** | Complete tear with defect gap > 6 cm |

## 5. Radiological Investigations

### Plain Radiographs for Achilles Rupture
* **Views:** AP & Lateral Ankle.
* **Purpose:** R/O avulsion fracture of calcaneal tuberosity, R/O ankle #.
* **Soft Tissue Signs:**
  * Obliteration of **Kager's fat pad** (Kager's triangle).
  * Thickening/irregularity of the posterior soft tissue shadow.

### Ultrasound (USG) for Achilles Rupture
* **Modality:** First-line, cheap, quick, dynamic assessment.
* **Findings:** Identifies full vs partial tear.
* **Dynamic Assessment:** Measures gap distance when ankle is placed in full equinus (plantarflexion) to guide conservative management suitability.

### MRI for Achilles Rupture
* **Modality:** Gold standard for soft tissue detail.
* **Indications:** Chronic/missed ruptures (>4-6 weeks), equivocal USG, complex proximal/musculotendinous junction tears.
* **Findings:** T1/T2 signal hyperintensity, tendon retraction, mucoid degeneration.

## 6. Initial ED Management

### ED Acute Interventions for Achilles Rupture
* **Immobilization:** Strict **Below-Knee (BK) Plaster of Paris (POP) backslab** or functional orthosis (VACOped) in **full equinus (maximum plantarflexion)** to approximate tendon ends.
* **Weight-bearing Status:** Strict **Non-Weight Bearing (NWB)** with crutches.
* **Analgesia:** PO Paracetamol/NSAIDs.
* **Discipline:** NBM if awaiting acute surgical intervention.
* **VTE Assessment:** Assess need for LMWH thromboprophylaxis based on HK HA guidelines (Caprini score).

## 7. Definitive Management Algorithm

### Non-Operative vs. Operative Indications for Achilles Rupture
| Management | Indications |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Non-Operative (Functional Rehab)** | Older/sedentary pts, significant comorbidities (DM, PVD, smokers), gap closure verified on equinus USG (<1cm gap). |
| **Operative (Surgical Repair)** | Young/active pts, high-demand athletes, delayed presentations (>2-4 weeks), re-ruptures, failed apposition in equinus. |

### Operative Techniques for Achilles Rupture
* **Open Repair:** 
  * Medial paratendinous incision (avoids sural nerve).
  * Direct end-to-end anastomosis using heavy non-absorbable sutures (e.g., **Krackow** or Kessler locking core stitch) + epitendinous repair.
* **Minimally Invasive / Percutaneous Repair (e.g., Achillon system):**
  * Stab incisions, jig-guided sutures. 
  * ↓ wound complications, but ↑ risk of iatrogenic **sural nerve** injury.
* **Augmentation (for chronic/Types III-IV):**
  * V-Y advancement, FHL transfer, or Peroneus brevis transfer.

## 8. Rehabilitation Protocol

### Functional Rehabilitation Protocol for Achilles Rupture
* **Phase 1 (0-2 weeks):** NWB in equinus cast or locked VACOped boot at 30° plantarflexion.
* **Phase 2 (2-6 weeks):** 
  * Transition to weight-bearing as tolerated (WBAT) in boot.
  * Boot angle gradually reduced (e.g., 10° dorsiflexion adjustment every 1-2 weeks) until neutral (0°).
  * Early active plantarflexion exercises; strictly NO active/passive dorsiflexion past neutral.
* **Phase 3 (6-12 weeks):** Wean off boot. Start intrinsic foot muscle strengthening, double-leg heel raises.
* **Phase 4 (>3-6 months):** Return to running (approx 4-6 months) and contact sports (approx 6-9 months) based on functional testing.

## 9. Orthopaedic Complications

### Surgical Complications of Achilles Rupture
* **Wound Breakdown & Infection:** Highest risk in open repair (thin soft tissue envelope, poor vascularity). ↑ in smokers/DM.
* **Sural Nerve Neuropathy:** Neuroma, dysesthesia on lateral foot. ↑ in percutaneous repair.
* **Tendon Tethering:** Scar tissue adhesion to skin.

### Non-Surgical & General Complications of Achilles Rupture
* **Re-Rupture:** Historically higher in non-op (10-12%) vs op (2-4%). Modern functional rehab protocols have equalized non-op re-rupture rates to match surgical rates.
* **Calf Atrophy & Weakness:** Permanent loss of extreme plantarflexion power (elongation of tendon).
* **VTE (DVT/PE):** High risk due to strict immobilization of the calf muscle pump.

## 10. Examiner's Pearls

* Trap: Assuming a patient does not have an Achilles rupture because they can actively plantarflex their foot. Pearl: The FHL, FDL, Tibialis Posterior, and Peroneus muscles can mask the loss of Achilles function; always rely on the Thompson squeeze test.
* Viva Question: Why does the Achilles tendon typically rupture 2 to 6 cm proximal to its insertion on the calcaneus? Answer: This region is a vascular watershed zone with the poorest blood supply, making it prone to degeneration and mechanical failure.
* Pitfall: Recommending open surgical repair for all young patients blindly. Evidence shows that with early dynamic functional rehabilitation and a closely monitored VACOped protocol, non-operative management yields similar re-rupture rates with zero surgical wound risks.
* Viva Question: During a percutaneous Achilles tendon repair, which nerve is most at risk and where does it supply sensation? Answer: The sural nerve (腓腸神經), which supplies sensation to the posterolateral aspect of the distal third of the leg and the lateral aspect of the foot.
* Trap: Placing the initial ED plaster cast in a neutral 90-degree ankle position. Pearl: An Achilles rupture MUST be splinted in maximum equinus (plantarflexion) to approximate the torn tendon ends and prevent gap formation.
