# Antepartum Haemorrhage (產前出血)

## 1. Clinical Definition, Gestational Context & Local Epidemiology

### Definition of Antepartum Haemorrhage
* **Antepartum haemorrhage (APH, 產前出血):** vaginal bleeding from **24 weeks’ gestation** until delivery
* Excludes bleeding after birth and bleeding before 24 weeks

### Key Local Causes of APH in Hong Kong
* Common emergency presentation in HA obstetric units
* Major causes: **placenta praevia**, **placental abruption**, local genital tract lesions, and **vasa praevia**
* Significant cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity, especially when associated with shock, preterm delivery, or fetal compromise

## 2. Pathophysiology, Endocrine Axis & Pelvic Anatomy (Maternal-Fetal Physiology)

### Pathophysiology of Placenta Praevia
* Abnormal low placentation over or near the internal cervical os
* Lower segment stretching and cervical effacement → shearing of placental bed vessels → painless bleeding

### Pathophysiology of Placental Abruption
* Premature separation of a normally sited placenta
* Retroplacental bleeding → uterine tenderness, hypertonus, and fetal hypoxia
* May be associated with:
  * Hypertension/pre-eclampsia
  * Trauma
  * Cocaine use
  * Smoking
  * Preterm prelabour rupture of membranes
  * Sudden decompression of polyhydramnios

### Pathophysiology of Vasa Praevia
* Fetal vessels run unsupported across the membranes over the internal os
* Vessel rupture causes **fetal bleeding**, which may rapidly lead to fetal exsanguination and death

## 3. Clinical Assessment (History, Abdominal, Speculum & Bimanual Exam Findings ONLY)

### History Features in APH
* Gestational age
* Painless or painful bleeding
* Painless bright-red bleeding suggests **placenta praevia**
* Painful dark bleeding with contractions suggests **abruption**
* Triggering events: trauma, fall, road traffic accident
* Amount of bleeding, clots, and sanitary pad count
* Associated symptoms:
  * Abdominal pain
  * Uterine contractions
  * Reduced fetal movements
  * Dizziness or syncope
* Previous APH, caesarean section, uterine scar, or known placenta praevia
* Antenatal ultrasound findings and placental site

### Abdominal Findings in APH
* Vital signs: hypotension, tachycardia, shock
* Uterine size and gestational age consistency
* **Tenderness, guarding, or hypertonus** suggests abruption
* High, non-engaged presenting part suggests placenta praevia
* Abnormal lie or malpresentation
* Fetal heart rate assessment

### Speculum Assessment in APH
* Inspect for local causes:
  * Cervical polyp
  * Cervical cancer
  * Cervicitis
  * Vaginal trauma
* Assess whether blood is coming through the cervical os
* Determine whether the cervix is open or closed
* **Do not perform digital vaginal or rectal examination until placenta praevia is excluded**

## 4. Critical Differential Diagnoses & Clinical Staging

### Major Differentials of APH
| Cause | Key Feature |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Placenta praevia (前置胎盤)** | Painless vaginal bleeding; high presenting part |
| **Placental abruption (胎盤早剝)** | Painful bleeding; tender/hypertonic uterus |
| **Vasa praevia (前置血管)** | Rupture of membranes with fetal bleeding/distress |
| **Local genital tract lesion** | Visible cervical/vaginal lesion |
| **Uterine rupture (子宮破裂)** | Previous uterine scar; pain, abnormal CTG, loss of station |
| **Show (見紅)** | Blood-stained mucus plug near labour |

### Classification of Placenta Praevia
* **Type I:** low-lying placenta in lower segment but not reaching internal os
* **Type II:** placenta reaches internal os but does not cover it
* **Type III:** placenta covers internal os asymmetrically
* **Type IV:** placenta centrally covers internal os

## 5. Key Investigations (Labs, Specific Ultrasound Criteria & CTG Tracings)

### Maternal Investigations in APH
* Full blood count
* Blood group and Rhesus status
* Cross-match if bleeding is moderate/severe
* Coagulation profile and fibrinogen if abruption or massive haemorrhage suspected
* Renal and liver function tests if clinically indicated
* Kleihauer test if significant fetomaternal haemorrhage suspected

### Ultrasound Findings in APH
* Assess placental site and distance from internal os
* Look for retroplacental clot in abruption
* Assess fetal presentation and estimated fetal weight
* Assess amniotic fluid volume
* Retroplacental clot is seen in only ~25% of abruptions; normal ultrasound does **not** exclude abruption

### Fetal Assessment in APH
* Continuous CTG if viable fetus
* Abruption may show:
  * Tachycardia
  * Reduced variability
  * Late decelerations
  * Sinusoidal pattern if severe fetal anaemia
  * Bradycardia in acute compromise

## 6. Medical Management & Acute Stabilization (Pharmacology, Steroids, Resuscitation)

### Acute Resuscitation in APH
* Call for obstetric, anaesthetic, and neonatal support if severe
* Left lateral tilt if viable pregnancy
* High-flow oxygen if hypoxic
* Wide-bore IV access
* Crystalloid resuscitation
* Cross-match blood early
* Catheterise bladder and monitor urine output
* Monitor BP, pulse, SpO₂, and conscious level

### Blood Products & Anti-D in APH
* Transfusion if haemodynamically unstable or symptomatic anaemia
* Fresh frozen plasma, platelets, cryoprecipitate if coagulopathy develops
* **Anti-D immunoglobulin** if Rh-negative mother and bleeding after 12 weeks

### Preterm Birth Pharmacotherapy in APH
* **Betamethasone** 12 mg IM 24-hourly × 2 doses if birth is likely before 34-37 weeks
* **Magnesium sulphate** for neuroprotection if delivery before 30-32 weeks
* Tocolysis generally avoided if abruption, active severe bleeding, or fetal compromise

## 7. Definitive Management Algorithm (Timing/Mode of Delivery OR Gynaecological Surgery)

### Admission & Monitoring in APH
* Admit unless bleeding is very mild and clearly from a local genital tract lesion
* Continuous maternal and fetal monitoring if severe APH
* Keep nil by mouth if delivery possible

### Timing of Delivery in APH
* **Immediate delivery** if:
  * Maternal haemodynamic instability
  * Heavy uncontrolled bleeding
  * Fetal distress
  * Suspected uterine rupture
  * Severe abruption
* **Aim for delivery around 37-38 weeks** in stable placenta praevia
* Deliver earlier if recurrent bleeding, labour, or fetal compromise
* Abruption with intrauterine death usually favours vaginal delivery if maternal condition is stable

### Mode of Delivery in APH
* **Caesarean section** for:
  * Major placenta praevia
  * Vasa praevia
  * Moderate-severe abruption with live fetus
  * Fetal distress
  * Abnormal lie or high presenting part
* **Vaginal delivery** may be appropriate for:
  * Mild abruption with reassuring fetal status
  * Local genital tract cause
  * Abruption with intrauterine death and stable mother
  * APH of unknown origin if labour is normal

## 8. Post-partum/Post-operative Care, Contraception & Follow-up

### Immediate Postpartum Care After APH
* Active management of third stage to prevent **postpartum haemorrhage**
* Oxytocin infusion after delivery
* Avoid ergometrine if hypertension is present
* Monitor for:
  * Uterine atony
  * Retained placenta
  * Coagulopathy
  * Renal impairment
* Blood transfusion and correction of anaemia as needed
* Thromboprophylaxis if risk factors present

### Follow-up After APH
* Placenta praevia:
  * Increased recurrence risk in future pregnancy
  * Document caesarean section details
  * Anticipate abnormal placentation, especially after previous caesarean section
* Abruption:
  * Counsel on recurrence risk
  * Address hypertension, smoking, and substance misuse
* Arrange postnatal review, haemoglobin check, and contraception discussion

## 9. Maternal, Fetal & Gynaecological Complications

### Maternal Complications of APH
* Hypovolaemic shock
* Disseminated intravascular coagulation
* Acute kidney injury
* Postpartum haemorrhage
* Hysterectomy in uncontrollable haemorrhage
* Maternal death

### Fetal & Neonatal Complications of APH
* Preterm birth
* Fetal growth restriction
* Fetal hypoxia or asphyxia
* Fetal anaemia, especially vasa praevia
* Intrauterine death
* Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome

## 10. Examiner's Pearls

* Viva Question: How would you distinguish placental abruption from placenta praevia in a stable patient? Answer: Abruption tends to cause pain, uterine tenderness, and hypertonus; praevia causes painless bleeding with a high presenting part, confirmed by ultrasound and clinical assessment.
* Trap: Using a normal ultrasound to exclude abruption. Pearl: Abruption remains a clinical diagnosis because ultrasound sensitivity is limited.
* Pitfall: Forgetting anti-D in an Rh-negative woman after significant APH. Give anti-D and consider Kleihauer testing to quantify fetomaternal haemorrhage.
* Viva Question: When would you choose immediate caesarean section in abruption with a live fetus? Answer: If there is fetal compromise, severe abruption, abnormal CTG, or failure of labour to progress; stable cases may still be managed vaginally if maternal and fetal status allow.
* Trap: Treating every APH as an obstetric emergency requiring immediate delivery. Pearl: Local genital tract lesions and stable praevia may be managed expectantly with close monitoring.
