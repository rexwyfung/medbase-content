# Vulvovaginal Cancer (外陰及陰道癌)

## 1. Clinical Definition, Gestational Context & Local Epidemiology

### Clinical Definition
* **Vulval Cancer (外陰癌)**: Primary malignant neoplasm arising from the external female genitalia (labia majora, labia minora, clitoris, Bartholin's glands).
* **Vaginal Cancer (陰道癌)**: Primary malignancy arising in the vaginal mucosa without involvement of the external os of the cervix or the vulva.

### Local Epidemiology (Hong Kong Context)
* **Incidence**: Rare in Hong Kong Hospital Authority (HA) records.
* **Vulval CA**: ~3-5% of all gynaecological malignancies.
* **Vaginal CA**: ~1-2% of all gynaecological malignancies (metastatic spread to vagina is far more common than primary vaginal cancer).
* **Peak Age**: Predominantly affects postmenopausal women (60–80 years).
* **Trend**: Increasing incidence of HPV-related vulvovaginal intraepithelial neoplasia in younger HK demographics.


## 2. Pathophysiology, Endocrine Axis & Pelvic Anatomy (Maternal-Fetal Physiology)

### Vulvovaginal Anatomy & Lymphatic Spread
* **Vulval Lymphatics**: Drain primarily to **inguinal-femoral lymph nodes (腹股溝淋巴結)** → deep pelvic nodes. 
* **Midline Vulval Lesions**: Bilateral lymphatic drainage (high risk of bilateral nodal mets).
* **Upper 1/3 Vagina**: Drains to pelvic nodes (obturator, internal/external iliac).
* **Lower 1/3 Vagina**: Drains to inguinal-femoral nodes.

### Pathophysiology & Histology
* **Squamous Cell Carcinoma (SCC) (鱗狀細胞癌)**: >90% of both vulval and vaginal cancers.
* **Melanoma (黑色素瘤)**: Second most common vulval cancer; high mortality.
* **Clear Cell Adenocarcinoma**: Linked to in-utero DES exposure (historically) or vaginal adenosis.

### Bimodal Aetiology (Vulval SCC)
* **HPV-Associated**: Younger women, linked to high-risk HPV 16/18, smoking, and Vulval High-Grade Squamous Intraepithelial Lesion (VHSIL).
* **Non-HPV Associated**: Older women, linked to chronic inflammatory dermatoses [e.g., **Lichen Sclerosus (硬化性苔蘚)**], leading to differentiated Vulval Intraepithelial Neoplasia (dVIN).


## 3. Clinical Assessment (History, Abdominal, Speculum & Bimanual Exam Findings ONLY)

### Maternal / Gynaecological History
* **Pruritus Vulvae**: Chronic, unrelenting vulval itch (most common symptom).
* **Mass/Ulcer**: Patient reports a palpable lump or non-healing ulcer.
* **Bleeding**: Postmenopausal bleeding or blood-stained vaginal discharge.
* **Pain**: Dyspareunia, dysuria, or localized vulval pain.

### Abdominal Exam Findings
* **Groin Palpation**: Enlarged, hard, fixed inguinal lymph nodes.
* **Lower Abdomen**: Usually unremarkable unless advanced pelvic mass is present.

### Speculum Exam Findings
* **Vaginal Walls**: Exophytic, ulcerated, or indurated mass (commonly in the upper posterior wall for vaginal cancer).
* **Cervix**: Must be examined to rule out primary cervical cancer extending to the vagina.

### Bimanual & Vulval Exam Findings
* **Vulval Inspection**: White, red, or pigmented plaques; fleshy nodule; or deep ulceration with rolled edges (commonly on labia majora).
* **Bimanual Palpation**: Assess fixity of the vaginal/vulval mass to underlying pelvic bones, urethra, or rectum.
* **Rectovaginal Exam**: Assess posterior vaginal wall involvement and rectal mucosa integrity.


## 4. Critical Differential Diagnoses & Clinical Staging (e.g., FIGO if applicable)

### Critical Differential Diagnoses
* **Vulval Dystrophies**: **Lichen Sclerosus (硬化性苔蘚)**.
* **Pre-malignant Lesions**: VIN, VAIN, Paget's disease of the vulva.
* **Benign Cysts**: Bartholin's gland cyst/abscess.
* **Infections**: Condylomata acuminata (genital warts), primary syphilis (chancre), herpes simplex ulceration.

### FIGO Staging for Vulval Cancer (2021)

| Stage | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Stage I** | Tumour confined to vulva/perineum; -ve nodes. (IA: $\le2$ cm, stromal invasion $\le1$ mm; IB: $>2$ cm or invasion $>1$ mm) |
| **Stage II** | Tumour extends to lower 1/3 urethra, lower 1/3 vagina, or anus; -ve nodes. |
| **Stage III** | +ve inguino-femoral lymph nodes. (IIIA: 1 node $\ge5$ mm or 1-2 nodes $<5$ mm; IIIB: $\ge2$ nodes $\ge5$ mm or $\ge3$ nodes $<5$ mm; IIIC: +ve extracapsular spread) |
| **Stage IV** | Tumour invades upper 2/3 urethra/vagina, bladder mucosa, rectal mucosa, or fixed to pelvic bone (IVA); OR distant metastasis (IVB). |

### FIGO Staging for Vaginal Cancer

| Stage | Description |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Stage I** | Tumour confined to the vaginal wall. |
| **Stage II** | Tumour invades subvaginal tissue but does not extend to the pelvic wall. |
| **Stage III** | Tumour extends to the pelvic wall. |
| **Stage IV** | Tumour invades bladder/rectal mucosa and/or extends beyond the true pelvis (IVA) or distant metastasis (IVB). |


## 5. Key Investigations (Labs, Specific Ultrasound Criteria & CTG Tracings)

### Tissue Diagnosis Criteria
* **Punch Biopsy**: Definitive diagnostic tool for any suspicious vulval ulcer/mass. Requires inclusion of the margin of normal skin and abnormal tissue.
* **Incisional/Wedge Biopsy**: For larger vaginal masses to obtain histological confirmation.

### Specific Imaging Criteria
* **MRI Pelvis**: Gold standard to assess primary tumour size, depth of stromal invasion, local organ involvement (bladder/rectum), and pelvic lymphadenopathy.
* **PET-CT Scan**: Crucial for detecting distant metastasis and assessing suspicious groin/pelvic nodes.

### Laboratory Results
* **CBP**: ↑ WBC if superinfected, ↓ Hb if chronic bleeding.
* **RFT / LFT**: Baseline required prior to contrast imaging and systemic therapy. Baseline creatinine crucial to rule out ureteric obstruction (hydronephrosis).
* **HPV DNA Testing**: Identifies high-risk strains (16, 18) for prognostic stratification.

### CTG Tracings
* **Not Applicable**: Usually a disease of postmenopausal women. If diagnosed during pregnancy (extremely rare), CTG is used strictly for standard fetal wellbeing monitoring post-viability, not for cancer diagnosis.


## 6. Medical Management & Acute Stabilization (Pharmacology, Steroids, Resuscitation)

### Acute Resuscitation & Stabilization
* **Haemostasis**: Vaginal packing with adrenaline-soaked gauze for acute vaginal tumour hemorrhage.
* **Intravenous Access**: Insert large-bore IV cannulae for fluid resuscitation if patient presents with hypovolaemic shock from massive bleeding.

### Pharmacological Management
* **Antifibrinolytics**: IV/PO Tranexamic acid to minimize ongoing bleeding.
* **Antibiotics**: Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (e.g., Augmentin + Metronidazole) for fungating, necrotic, superinfected vulval masses.
* **Analgesia**: Step 3 WHO pain ladder (oral/IV morphine) for severe cancer-related pelvic/perineal pain.

### Symptom Control (Dermatological)
* **Topical Corticosteroids**: Ultrapotent steroids (e.g., Clobetasol) for adjacent areas of severe Lichen Sclerosus to reduce extreme pruritus.
* **Barrier Creams**: Zinc oxide to protect surrounding healthy skin from excoriation and discharge.


## 7. Definitive Management Algorithm (Timing/Mode of Delivery OR Gynaecological Surgery)

### Vulval Cancer Definitive Surgery
* **Wide Local Excision (WLE)**: For unifocal T1a lesions. Must achieve surgical margins $\ge1$ cm.
* **Radical Local Excision / Radical Vulvectomy**: For >T1a lesions. Deep margin must reach the inferior fascia of the urogenital diaphragm.
* **Groin Lymph Node Assessment**:
    * **Sentinel Lymph Node (SLN) Biopsy**: Used for early-stage ($<4$ cm), unifocal tumours with clinically negative groins. Uses Technetium-99m and blue dye/ICG.
    * **Inguino-femoral Lymphadenectomy**: Standard for larger tumours. Bilateral dissection required if the lesion is within 1 cm of the midline.

### Vaginal Cancer Definitive Management
* **Primary Radiotherapy**: Treatment of choice for the vast majority of vaginal cancers (External Beam Radiotherapy + Intracavitary Brachytherapy).
* **Surgical Resection (Radical Vaginectomy)**: Reserved only for early Stage I lesions confined to the upper posterior vaginal vault, often combined with radical hysterectomy.

### Adjuvant Therapy
* **Radiotherapy (Groin/Pelvis)**: Indicated if $\ge2$ positive groin nodes, extracapsular spread, or positive/close surgical margins ($<8$ mm pathological margin).
* **Chemoradiation**: Concurrent Cisplatin is given for advanced (Stage III/IV) vulval or vaginal cancers.


## 8. Post-partum/Post-operative Care, Contraception & Follow-up

### Post-Operative Care Protocol
* **Wound Care**: Daily irrigation of vulval wounds. Prevent maceration.
* **Drain Management**: Groin suction drains left in situ until output is $<30$ ml/24 hours to prevent lymphocysts.
* **DVT Prophylaxis**: Strict administration of Low Molecular Weight Heparin (LMWH) and TED stockings due to high pelvic thrombosis risk.
* **Catheterization**: Foley catheter remains for 3-5 days to prevent urine contamination of the vulval wound.

### Contraception & HRT
* **Contraception**: Not relevant for the typical postmenopausal demographic. If premenopausal, avoid high-dose estrogen if history of thromboembolism.
* **HRT**: Systemic HRT is generally safe for SCC survivors to manage severe menopausal symptoms.

### Oncology Follow-up Schedule (HA Standard)
* **Years 1-2**: Review every 3-4 months. Speculum exam, groin palpation.
* **Years 3-5**: Review every 6 months.
* **>5 Years**: Annual review. Continual surveillance of remaining vulval skin for new primary lesions (field cancerization effect).


## 9. Maternal, Fetal & Gynaecological Complications

### Gynaecological & Surgical Complications
* **Wound Breakdown**: Very common post-radical vulvectomy due to tension and poor vascularity.
* **Lymphoedema (淋巴水腫)**: Chronic, irreversible swelling of lower limbs post-inguinal lymphadenectomy.
* **Lymphocyst**: Fluid accumulation in the groin requiring prolonged drainage or sclerosis.
* **Sexual Dysfunction**: Dyspareunia, loss of clitoral sensation, altered body image.

### Radiotherapy Complications
* **Vaginal Stenosis**: Narrowing and shortening of the vagina (requires prophylactic use of vaginal dilators).
* **Radiation Proctitis/Cystitis**: Chronic rectal bleeding, strictures, or urinary frequency.
* **Fistula Formation**: Vesicovaginal or rectovaginal fistulas due to tissue necrosis.

### Pregnancy Complications (Rare)
* **Mode of Delivery**: Elective LSCS (剖腹產) is mandatory if the tumour obtrudes the birth canal or to prevent vaginal tearing through a vulval tumour.


## 10. Examiner's Pearls

* Viva Question: What is the most important prognostic factor for vulval squamous cell carcinoma? Answer: The status of the inguino-femoral lymph nodes.
* Viva Question: How does the lymphatic drainage dictate groin surgery in vulval cancer? Answer: Lateral lesions ($>1$ cm from midline) require unilateral dissection; midline lesions require bilateral dissection.
* Trap: Prescribing antifungal cream for a chronic, non-healing vulval ulcer in an elderly woman. Pearl: Any persistent vulval lesion or ulcer in a postmenopausal woman mandates a targeted punch biopsy to rule out SCC.
* Pitfall: Inadequate surgical margins during vulval excision. The surgeon must aim for macroscopic margins of $\ge15$ mm to ensure pathological margins are $>8$ mm, otherwise the risk of local recurrence increases dramatically.
* Trap: Assuming vaginal cancer is a primary lesion. Pearl: Primary vaginal cancer is a diagnosis of exclusion. You must strictly rule out metastasis from the cervix, endometrium, vulva, or bowel before diagnosing primary vaginal SCC.
