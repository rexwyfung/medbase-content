# Guillain-Barré Syndrome (格林-巴利綜合症)

## 1. Clinical Definition, Etiologies & Local Epidemiology

### Clinical Definition of GBS
* **Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS)**: Acute, immune-mediated, post-infectious polyneuropathy characterized by rapidly progressive, symmetric, ascending flaccid paralysis.
* **AIDP (Acute Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy)**: The most common phenotype in Western countries and Hong Kong.

### Common Infectious Triggers for GBS
* **Antecedent Infection**: Occurs 1-4 weeks prior to neurological onset in ~70% of cases.
* ***Campylobacter jejuni***: Most common trigger (~30%); strongly associated with axonal variants and severe disease.
* **Viruses**: **Cytomegalovirus (CMV)**, **Epstein-Barr Virus (EBV)**, **Zika virus**, **SARS-CoV-2**.
* **Bacteria**: *Mycoplasma pneumoniae*.
* **Vaccines**: Historically linked to older influenza vaccines; currently extremely rare/negligible risk.

### Hong Kong Epidemiology of GBS
* **Incidence**: ~1-2 per 100,000 person-years locally.
* **Demographics**: Slight male predominance; affects all ages but incidence ↑ with age.
* **Seasonality**: Higher peaks observed in late winter/spring locally, correlating with respiratory & GI infection peaks.

## 2. Systemic Pathophysiology & Molecular Mechanisms

### Molecular Mimicry in GBS
* Pathophysiology driven by **molecular mimicry**.
* Immune system generates cross-reactive antibodies against pathogen surface structures (e.g., *C. jejuni* lipooligosaccharides).
* These antibodies mistakenly target host **gangliosides** (glycolipids abundant in peripheral nerve myelin and axolemma).

### GBS Subtypes and Autoantibodies
| Subtype | Pathology Target | Clinical Features | Associated Autoantibody |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **AIDP** | Myelin sheath (Schwann cells) | Ascending weakness, sensory symptoms, demyelination | Non-specific / Unknown |
| **AMAN** | Axolemma (Nodes of Ranvier) | Motor ONLY, no sensory loss, severe, *Campylobacter* linked | **Anti-GM1**, **Anti-GD1a** |
| **AMSAN** | Axolemma (Motor + Sensory) | Severe sensorimotor axonal loss, slow/poor recovery | **Anti-GM1**, **Anti-GD1a** |
| **Miller Fisher Syndrome (MFS)** | Cranial nerves, NMJ, Muscle spindles | Triad: Ophthalmoplegia, Ataxia, Areflexia | **Anti-GQ1b** |

## 3. Clinical Assessment (Detailed History & System-Specific Physical Signs ONLY)

### History of Presenting Illness in GBS
* **Infectious Prodrome**: Diarrhea or URTI 1-4 weeks prior.
* **Weakness Pattern**: Symmetrical, **ascending** flaccid weakness (lower limbs → upper limbs → respiratory/facial muscles).
* **Sensory Symptoms**: Distal paresthesia/numbness ("rubbery legs", tingling fingers) usually preceding weakness.
* **Pain**: Severe back or radicular pain is extremely common early in the disease course.

### Neurological Examination Signs of GBS
* **Motor**: LMN pattern. Flaccid tone, symmetric weakness (often proximal > distal early on).
* **Reflexes**: **Areflexia** or profound hyporeflexia (hallmark sign, required for classic Dx).
* **Sensory**: Mild "glove-and-stocking" loss (large fiber: vibration/proprioception > small fiber: pinprick).
* **Cranial Nerves (CN)**: 
  * Bilateral lower motor neuron **Facial Nerve (CN VII)** palsy.
  * Bulbar palsy (CN IX, X, XII) → dysarthria, dysphagia, absent gag.
  * Ophthalmoplegia (in Miller Fisher variant).

### Autonomic Instability Signs in GBS
* **Cardiovascular**: Arrhythmias (sinus tachycardia, marked bradycardia, heart block), labile blood pressure (orthostatic hypotension alternating with severe hypertension).
* **Gastrointestinal**: Adynamic ileus.
* **Genitourinary**: Urinary retention.
* **Sudomotor**: Loss of sweating / anhidrosis.

## 4. Differential Diagnoses & Discriminatory Features

### Differentials for Acute Flaccid Weakness
| Diagnosis | Key Discriminatory Features vs GBS |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Spinal Cord Compression / Transverse Myelitis** | Prominent sensory level, early bowel/bladder dysfunction, UMN signs (hyperreflexia, Babinski +ve) develop later. |
| **Myasthenia Gravis (MG)** | Fluctuating weakness, fatiguability, **normal reflexes**, no sensory loss. |
| **Botulism** | **Descending** paralysis, prominent dilated unreactive pupils, early bulbar signs. |
| **Hypokalemic Periodic Paralysis** | History of thyrotoxicosis/carb loading, ↓ K+ on bloods, normal CSF. |
| **CIDP (Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy)** | Progression duration **> 8 weeks** (GBS nadir is always ≤ 4 weeks). |
| **Inflammatory Myopathy (Polymyositis)** | Preserved reflexes, proximal weakness only, ↑ CK, no sensory loss. |

## 5. Tiered Investigations (Bedside, Bloods, Imaging, Biopsy & Diagnostic Criteria)

### Bedside Investigations for GBS
* **Forced Vital Capacity (FVC)**: Most critical bedside test. serial monitoring (Q4H) to detect impending respiratory failure.
* **Single Breath Count**: Surrogate marker if spirometry unavailable (count < 20 indicates ↓ vital capacity).
* **ECG**: Crucial for detecting autonomic-driven arrhythmias (heart block, extreme tachy/bradycardia).

### Blood Tests for GBS
* **Basic Panel**: LFT, U&E, CK, Ca/Mg/PO4 (exclude metabolic mimics like hypokalemia).
* **Autoantibody Panel**: **Anti-GQ1b** (MFS), **Anti-GM1/GD1a** (AMAN).
* **Infectious Serology**: *Campylobacter*, CMV, EBV, HIV (often academic, doesn't change acute Rx).

### Cerebrospinal Fluid (CSF) Analysis in GBS
* **Lumbar Puncture (LP)**: Required to demonstrate **Albuminocytological Dissociation**.
* **Findings**: ↑ Protein (>0.55 g/L) with normal WCC (<5-10 cells/mm³).
* **Timing caveat**: May be completely normal in the first 1-2 weeks; repeating LP in week 2/3 ↑ yield. If marked pleocytosis (>50 cells), suspect HIV, Lyme, or sarcoidosis.

### Nerve Conduction Studies (NCS) / EMG in GBS
* **Utility**: Confirms Dx and distinguishes AIDP from AMAN/AMSAN.
* **Demyelinating (AIDP) Findings**: ↓ Conduction velocity, prolonged distal motor latencies, temporal dispersion, **conduction blocks**, prolonged or absent **F-waves** (earliest sign indicating nerve root demyelination).
* **Axonal (AMAN) Findings**: ↓ Compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitudes with preserved velocities.
* **Timing caveat**: Can be normal in the first 7-10 days.

### Brighton Diagnostic Criteria for GBS
| Criteria Component | Requirement |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Clinical Pattern** | Bilateral, flaccid weakness of limbs + decreased/absent deep tendon reflexes. |
| **Disease Course** | Monophasic pattern, reaching nadir between 12 hours and 28 days (4 weeks). |
| **Alternative Dx** | Exclusion of mimics (myasthenia, botulism, cord compression). |
| **Supportive Evidence** | CSF albuminocytological dissociation OR Electrophysiological (NCS) features consistent with GBS. |

## 6. Acute Exacerbation Management & Resuscitation (Flares/Emergencies)

### Respiratory Monitoring & Intubation Criteria for GBS
* **Monitoring Protocol**: Strict serial FVC & Maximal Inspiratory/Expiratory Pressures (MIP/MEP).
* **ICU Admission & "20-30-40 Rule" for Intubation**:
  * Vital Capacity **< 20 mL/kg** (or rapidly falling to < 1 - 1.5 L).
  * MIP **< 30 cm H₂O**.
  * MEP **< 40 cm H₂O**.
* **Other Intubation Triggers**: Bulbar weakness (severe aspiration risk), rapid >30% drop in FVC, dysautonomia with hemodynamic collapse.

### Autonomic Dysreflexia Resuscitation in GBS
* **Setting**: Continuous telemetry in High Dependency Unit (HDU) or ICU; arterial line for continuous BP monitoring.
* **Bradycardia/Asystole**: IV Atropine; temporary transvenous pacing if refractory.
* **Hypotension**: IV fluid resuscitation; use vasopressors with extreme caution (patients are highly sensitive due to denervation hypersensitivity).
* **Hypertension**: Avoid long-acting agents. Use short-acting, titratable IV agents (e.g., Labetalol, Esmolol).

### Acute Immunomodulatory Therapy for GBS
* **Indication**: Non-ambulatory patients, rapidly progressive weakness, bulbar/respiratory involvement. Must start within 2 (up to 4) weeks of symptom onset.
* **Option 1: Intravenous Immunoglobulin (IVIG)** 
  * **Dose**: 0.4 g/kg/day for 5 days. 
  * Preferred in HK HA due to ease of use and lower complication profile vs PLEX.
* **Option 2: Plasmapheresis (PLEX)**
  * **Dose**: 5 plasma exchanges over 7-14 days.
  * Equivalent efficacy to IVIG. 
* **Rule**: Do NOT combine IVIG and PLEX (PLEX will wash out the IVIG; combined use lacks proven additive benefit).
* **Contraindicated**: Corticosteroids (systematic reviews show no benefit; may worsen outcome).

## 7. Chronic Management Algorithm & Disease-Modifying Therapies (Pharmacology & Lifestyle)

### Distinction and Transition from GBS to CIDP
* GBS is strictly a monophasic illness (nadir ≤ 4 weeks).
* If progressive weakness continues **> 8 weeks**, or if there are relapses after initial improvement, the diagnosis shifts to **CIDP**.
* **CIDP Chronic Rx**: Long-term IVIG, Plasmapheresis, Corticosteroids, or immunosuppressants (e.g., Azathioprine, Mycophenolate) [Note: Steroids work for CIDP, NOT GBS].

### Neuropathic Pain Management in GBS
* **1st Line**: Gabapentin (加巴噴丁) or Pregabalin (普瑞巴林).
* **2nd Line**: TCAs e.g., Amitriptyline (阿米替林) or SNRI e.g., Duloxetine (度洛西汀).
* **Acute flares**: Short-term NSAIDs or mild opioids (beware exacerbating ileus/constipation).

### Complication Prophylaxis in GBS
* **VTE Prophylaxis**: LMWH (e.g., Enoxaparin) SC daily + Graduated compression stockings (TEDs) / sequential compression devices until patient regains independent mobility.
* **Pressure Area Care**: 2-hourly turning routine, pressure-relieving air mattress.
* **Eye Care**: Lubricating eye drops/taping for lagophthalmos (facial nerve palsy) to prevent exposure keratopathy.

### Rehabilitation and Multidisciplinary Care for GBS
* **Physiotherapy (PT)**: Graded motor retraining, passive range-of-motion to prevent joint contractures.
* **Occupational Therapy (OT)**: ADL adaptations, orthoses (e.g., Ankle-Foot Orthosis [AFO] for residual foot drop).
* **Speech Therapy (ST)**: Swallowing assessment (FEES / videofluoroscopy) to guide safe oral feeding vs prolonged NGT/PEG.

## 8. Risk Stratification, Clinical Scoring Systems & Prognosis

### EGRIS (Erasmus GBS Respiratory Insufficiency Score)
* **Purpose**: Predicts the probability of requiring mechanical ventilation within 1 week of admission.
* **Variables (0-7 points)**:
  1. Days between symptom onset and admission.
  2. Presence of facial and/or bulbar weakness.
  3. MRC sum score (Medical Research Council muscle grading).

### EGOS (Erasmus GBS Outcome Score)
* **Purpose**: Predicts the probability of being unable to walk independently at 6 months.
* **Variables (assessed at 2 weeks post-admission)**:
  1. Age (>60 carries worse prognosis).
  2. Presence of preceding diarrhea (*Campylobacter*).
  3. MRC sum score at 2 weeks.

### Prognosis & Recovery Timelines for GBS
* **Progression Phase**: Up to 4 weeks (most nadir by week 2).
* **Plateau Phase**: Lasts days to weeks.
* **Recovery Phase**: Remyelination and axonal regeneration takes weeks to months (axonal growth is ~1mm/day).
* **Outcomes**: ~80% can walk independently at 6 months. ~60% suffer residual fatigue/neuropathic pain.
* **Mortality**: 3-5% (usually due to autonomic arrhythmias, VAP, or massive PE).

## 9. Multi-System Complications (End-Organ Damage & Drug Adverse Effects)

### Neurological and Systemic Complications in GBS
* **Neurological**: Permanent axonal damage → persistent distal weakness (foot drop), chronic painful neuropathy.
* **Respiratory**: Ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), tracheomalacia (from prolonged intubation).
* **Cardiovascular**: Fatal arrhythmias, Pulmonary Embolism (DVT propagation).
* **GI/GU**: Severe paralytic ileus (may lead to bowel perforation), catheter-associated UTIs.

### IVIG Adverse Effects
* **Anaphylaxis**: High risk in patients with underlying **IgA deficiency**.
* **Neurological**: Aseptic meningitis (severe headache, photophobia post-infusion).
* **Renal**: Acute Kidney Injury (AKI) due to osmotic nephrosis (especially with older sucrose-containing preparations).
* **Hematological**: Hyperviscosity leading to thromboembolism (Stroke, DVT).

### Plasmapheresis (PLEX) Adverse Effects
* **Metabolic**: Hypocalcemia (citrate toxicity from the anticoagulant used in lines → perioral tingling, tetany).
* **Cardiovascular**: Fluid shifts causing severe hypotension.
* **Infectious**: Line-associated bloodstream infections (CLABSI).
* **Hematological**: Coagulopathy (depletion of clotting factors).

## 10. Examiner's Pearls

* Viva Question: What is the most important bedside test for a newly admitted GBS patient and why? Answer: Serial FVC (Forced Vital Capacity); it detects impending diaphragmatic failure before oxygen desaturation or hypercapnia occurs.
* Trap: Assuming a normal CSF protein rules out GBS. Pearl: CSF albuminocytological dissociation is often absent in the first week of illness; LP should be repeated in 1-2 weeks if initial results are normal and suspicion remains high.
* Pitfall: Giving systemic corticosteroids to a patient with GBS. Systemic steroids have no proven benefit in acute GBS, do not speed recovery, and may actually delay nerve regeneration and increase myopathy risk.
* Viva Question: How do you differentiate clinically between the weakness of GBS and a high cervical spinal cord lesion? Answer: GBS causes LMN signs (flaccid, areflexia) without a distinct sensory level, whereas a cord lesion will rapidly develop UMN signs (Babinski), a strict dermatomal sensory level, and early bowel/bladder incontinence.
* Trap: Confusing AIDP with AMAN electrophysiologically. Pearl: AIDP shows demyelinating features (prolonged latencies, slow velocity, conduction block), while AMAN shows axonal features (decreased CMAP amplitude with preserved velocity).
* Pitfall: Rushing to intubate based solely on a single low oxygen saturation reading. Intubation in GBS is driven by mechanical failure criteria (the 20/30/40 rule of FVC/MIP/MEP) or bulbar failure, not just hypoxia, which is a very late and dangerous sign.
