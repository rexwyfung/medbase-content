# Tropical Infections (熱帶傳染病)

## 1. Clinical Definition, Etiologies & Local Epidemiology

### Tropical Infections Overview in Hong Kong
* **Context**: HK is subtropical, serving as an international travel hub. Tropical infections are either imported (travel-related) or locally endemic.
* **Statutory Notification**: Most severe tropical infections are HK Notifiable Diseases (香港法定傳染病) under Cap 599. Must report to Centre for Health Protection (CHP) (衛生防護中心).
* **Key Notifiable Tropical Diseases**: Dengue, Japanese Encephalitis (JE), Typhoid/Paratyphoid, Rabies, Zika, Chikungunya, Leptospirosis, Melioidosis (recently added in 2023).

### Dengue Fever (登革熱)
* **Pathogen**: Dengue virus (Flavivirus), 4 serotypes (DEN 1-4).
* **Vector**: *Aedes albopictus* (白紋伊蚊) (endemic in HK) & *Aedes aegypti* (埃及伊蚊).
* **HK Epi**: Primarily imported from SE Asia; occasional local indigenous clusters (e.g., Lion Rock Park outbreak).

### Scrub Typhus (叢林斑疹傷寒)
* **Pathogen**: *Orientia tsutsugamushi* (intracellular bacterium).
* **Vector**: Trombiculid mite larvae (Chiggers) (恙蟎).
* **HK Epi**: Significant local transmission in hikers/farmers exposed to scrub vegetation in New Territories/Lantau Island (usually May-Oct).

### Melioidosis (類鼻疽)
* **Pathogen**: *Burkholderia pseudomallei* (Gram -ve bacillus).
* **Source**: Contaminated soil/water (cutaneous inoculation, inhalation, ingestion).
* **HK Epi**: Endemic. Spikes after heavy rain/typhoons. Major recent local cluster in Sham Shui Po.

### Leptospirosis (鉤端螺旋體病)
* **Pathogen**: *Leptospira interrogans* (spirochete).
* **Source**: Contact with animal urine (rodents/dogs) in contaminated freshwater or wet soil.

### Enteric Fever: Typhoid & Paratyphoid (傷寒與副傷寒)
* **Pathogen**: *Salmonella enterica* serotypes Typhi & Paratyphi.
* **Transmission**: Fecal-oral, contaminated food/water.
* **HK Epi**: Highly imported from South Asia (India/Pakistan/Nepal/Bangladesh/Indonesia).

### Japanese Encephalitis (日本腦炎)
* **Pathogen**: JE virus (Flavivirus).
* **Vector**: *Culex tritaeniorhynchus* (三帶喙庫蚊) mosquitoes. Amplifying hosts: Pigs, wading birds.
* **HK Epi**: Rare local cases (usually near pig farms in Yuen Long/Tin Shui Wai).

### Rabies (狂犬病)
* **Pathogen**: Rabies virus (Lyssavirus/Rhabdoviridae).
* **Transmission**: Saliva via bite/scratch from infected mammals (dogs, bats).
* **HK Epi**: Animal rabies-free for >30 years. High risk in imported cases (e.g., bites in SE Asia/Mainland China).

### Chikungunya & Zika (基孔肯雅熱與寨卡病毒)
* **Pathogens**: Chikungunya (Alphavirus), Zika (Flavivirus).
* **Vector**: *Aedes* mosquitoes (same as Dengue).
* **Transmission**: Zika also sexual and vertical transmission.

## 2. Systemic Pathophysiology & Molecular Mechanisms

### Pathophysiology of Dengue & Severe Dengue
* **Antibody-Dependent Enhancement (ADE)**: 2nd infection with *different* serotype → pre-existing non-neutralizing Abs bind virus → ↑ viral entry into macrophages → massive cytokine storm.
* **Vascular Leakage**: Viral NS1 protein & cytokines (TNF-α) → endothelial glycocalyx disruption → transient ↑ capillary permeability → plasma leakage (pleural effusion, ascites) → Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS).
* **Coagulopathy**: Virus inhibits bone marrow (megakaryocytes) + immune destruction of platelets → profound thrombocytopenia & bleeding.

### Pathophysiology of Scrub Typhus
* **Target Cells**: Obligate intracellular infection of endothelial cells and macrophages.
* **Mechanism**: Widespread disseminated vasculitis/perivasculitis → microvascular leakage, tissue hypoperfusion, organ dysfunction (ARDS, AKI, meningoencephalitis).

### Pathophysiology of Melioidosis
* **Intracellular Survival**: Evades phagosome killing, induces multinucleated giant cell formation via Type VI secretion system.
* **Dissemination**: Spreads rapidly via lymphatics/blood → forms granulomatous microabscesses in virtually any organ (predilection for lungs, liver, spleen, prostate, brain).

### Pathophysiology of Leptospirosis & Weil's Disease
* **Invasion**: Penetrates intact mucous membranes or micro-abrasions in skin.
* **Systemic Phase**: Rapid bacteremic spread (first 5-7 days).
* **Immune Phase (Weil's Disease)**: Immune complex deposition → diffuse endothelitis → capillary damage in kidneys (Acute Tubular Necrosis), liver (centrilobular necrosis → jaundice without major hepatocellular necrosis), and lungs (Pulmonary Alveolar Hemorrhage).

### Pathophysiology of Enteric Fever (Typhoid)
* **Invasion**: Crosses intestinal epithelium via M cells in Peyer's patches.
* **Intracellular Replication**: Survives in macrophages → systemic dissemination via reticuloendothelial system (RES) (liver, spleen, bone marrow, gallbladder).
* **Hyperplasia**: Massive RES lymphoid hyperplasia → hepatosplenomegaly & terminal ileum ulceration/perforation (classically 3rd week of illness).

### Pathophysiology of Rabies
* **Neural Spread**: Virus binds nicotinic acetylcholine receptors at NMJ → retrograde axonal transport (via dynein) at 1-2 cm/day to DRG and CNS.
* **Encephalitis**: Massive viral replication in brain (Negri bodies) → neuronal dysfunction (without massive necrosis) → centrifugal spread to salivary glands.

## 3. Clinical Assessment

### Travel History "5 Ws" Approach
* **Who**: Age, comorbidities, immunocompromise, pregnancy, vaccination/chemoprophylaxis history.
* **Where**: Exact itinerary, rural vs urban, altitude, local outbreaks.
* **When**: Date of return relative to symptom onset (determines incubation period: <14 days vs >14 days).
* **What**: Exposures (freshwater, soil, raw food, insect bites, animal contacts, sexual history).
* **Why**: Tourism, visiting friends/relatives (VFRs at highest risk), medical tourism.

### Clinical Assessment for Dengue Fever
* **Incubation**: 4-10 days (Short).
* **Classical Symptoms**: Sudden onset high fever (biphasic/"saddleback"), intense retro-orbital pain, severe myalgia/arthralgia ("bone-break fever") (斷骨熱).
* **Skin Signs**: Tourniquet test +ve. Early flushing → late maculopapular rash with "islands of white in a sea of red" (convalescent rash), petechiae.
* **Warning Signs (History)**: Persistent vomiting, severe abdominal pain, mucosal bleeding, extreme lethargy/restlessness.
* **Warning Signs (Physical)**: Hepatomegaly >2cm, clinical fluid accumulation (ascites, pleural effusion), cold clammy skin (impending shock).

### Clinical Assessment for Scrub Typhus
* **Incubation**: 6-21 days.
* **Eschar (焦痂)**: Pathognomonic. Painless, punched-out ulcer with black necrotic center & erythematous halo. Must thoroughly inspect hidden areas (groin, axillae, inframammary folds, perineum).
* **Systemic Signs**: Fever, severe headache, generalized tender lymphadenopathy (esp. regional to eschar), maculopapular rash (centrifugal spread).

### Clinical Assessment for Melioidosis
* **Incubation**: Highly variable (days to decades - latency).
* **Pulmonary Form**: Most common. Productive cough, hemoptysis, pleuritic pain.
* **Localized/Septic Form**: Cutaneous abscesses, parotid swelling, prostatic tenderness (on PR exam), severe sepsis/septic shock out of proportion to focal signs.

### Clinical Assessment for Leptospirosis
* **Incubation**: 2-30 days.
* **Early Signs**: High fever, severe calf/lumbar myalgia.
* **Eye Signs**: Conjunctival suffusion (red eyes *without* purulent exudate) - highly characteristic.
* **Weil's Disease Signs**: Deep jaundice, oliguria (renal failure), mucosal hemorrhage, hemoptysis (alveolar hemorrhage).

### Clinical Assessment for Enteric Fever (Typhoid)
* **Incubation**: 7-14 days.
* **Week 1**: Step-ladder fever, severe headache, dry cough, constipation (more common than diarrhea initially).
* **Week 2**: Relative bradycardia (Faget's sign), Rose spots (blanching salmon-colored macules on trunk), hepatosplenomegaly, abdominal distension.
* **Week 3**: "Typhoid state" (mutism, apathy, delirium), signs of peritonitis (perforation), GI bleeding (melena).

### Clinical Assessment for Zika & Chikungunya
* **Zika**: Low-grade fever, non-purulent conjunctivitis, pruritic maculopapular rash, arthralgia.
* **Chikungunya**: Explosive onset fever, incapacitating/crippling polyarthralgia/polyarthritis (often bilateral, symmetric, distal joints), stooped posture.

### Clinical Assessment for Rabies
* **Incubation**: 1-3 months (depends on bite distance from brain).
* **Prodrome**: Pain/paresthesia/pruritus strictly at the healed bite site.
* **Encephalitic (Furious) Form**: Hydrophobia (spasms of pharyngeal muscles on seeing/trying to drink water), aerophobia, hypersalivation, hyperactivity, dysautonomia.
* **Paralytic (Dumb) Form**: Ascending flaccid paralysis (mimics Guillain-Barré).

## 4. Differential Diagnoses & Discriminatory Features

### Undifferentiated Fever in the Returning Traveler
| Diagnosis | Vector/Exposure | Discriminatory Clinical Features |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Malaria** | *Anopheles* | Fever (paroxysmal), rigors, splenomegaly, anemia. MUST EXCLUDE FIRST. |
| **Dengue** | *Aedes* | Retro-orbital pain, bone-break myalgia, early rash, bleeding. |
| **Typhoid** | Fecal-oral | Step-ladder fever, relative bradycardia, rose spots, prominent GI sx. |
| **Scrub Typhus** | Chigger (mite) | Pathognomonic eschar, regional lymphadenopathy, rural exposure. |
| **Leptospirosis** | Rodent urine/Water | Conjunctival suffusion, prominent calf myalgia, jaundice. |
| **Melioidosis** | Soil/Water | Pneumonia + abscesses (prostate/liver/spleen), severe sepsis. |
| **Zika** | *Aedes* | Non-purulent conjunctivitis, prominent pruritic rash. |
| **Chikungunya** | *Aedes* | Crippling/disabling polyarthritis (often persistent). |

### Rash Characteristics in Tropical Fevers
* **Dengue**: "Islands of white in a sea of red" (petechial/confluent), often itchy during recovery.
* **Typhoid**: Rose spots (sparse, blanching, salmon-colored macules on trunk).
* **Scrub Typhus**: Dull maculopapular rash spreading outward from trunk.

## 5. Tiered Investigations

### Diagnostic Investigations for Dengue
* **Bedside**: Tourniquet test (+ve if ≥10-20 petechiae per square inch).
* **Virology (Days 1-5)**: Dengue NS1 Antigen (serum) + Dengue RT-PCR.
* **Serology (Day >5)**: Dengue IgM/IgG ELISA.
* **Bloods (CBC Trends)**: Progressive leukopenia, severe thrombocytopenia.
* **Plasma Leakage Markers**: ↑ Hematocrit (≥20% rise defines plasma leak), ↓ Albumin.
* **Imaging**: CXR/Ultrasound (detect pleural effusion, ascites, gallbladder wall thickening/edema).

### Diagnostic Investigations for Scrub Typhus
* **Serology**: Indirect Immunofluorescence Assay (IFA) (Gold Standard). Weil-Felix test (historical, poor sensitivity).
* **Molecular**: PCR of eschar swab or whole blood (buffy coat).
* **Bloods**: Mild thrombocytopenia, mild ↑ Transaminases.

### Diagnostic Investigations for Melioidosis
* **Microbiology**: Blood, sputum, pus, urine cultures. MUST notify lab if suspected to use specific selective media (Ashdown's agar).
* **Pathology**: Gram -ve bacilli with "safety pin" appearance (bipolar staining). Ashdown's agar shows dry, wrinkled, purple colonies.
* **Imaging**: CT Abdomen/Pelvis (mandatory to screen for subclinical liver/spleen/prostatic abscesses).

### Diagnostic Investigations for Leptospirosis
* **Serology**: Microscopic Agglutination Test (MAT) (Gold standard, usually retrospective, requires paired sera). Leptospira IgM (screening).
* **Molecular**: Leptospira PCR (blood/CSF in week 1; urine in weeks 2-3).
* **Bloods**: ↑ Bilirubin (out of proportion to mild ↑ AST/ALT), ↑ CK (muscle damage), ↑ Creatinine (AKI), thrombocytopenia.

### Diagnostic Investigations for Enteric Fever (Typhoid)
* **Microbiology**:
  * Week 1: Blood culture (highest yield).
  * Week 2-3: Stool & Urine cultures.
  * Bone marrow culture: Highest sensitivity overall, remains +ve even after starting antibiotics.
* **Serology**: Widal test (detects O and H antigens) - historically used but lacks sensitivity/specificity in endemic areas; rarely relied upon now in HK.
* **Bloods**: Leukopenia, eosinopenia, ↑ AST/ALT.

### Diagnostic Criteria for Severe Dengue (WHO 2009)
* **Criteria 1**: Severe plasma leakage leading to Shock (DSS) or Fluid accumulation with respiratory distress.
* **Criteria 2**: Severe bleeding (as evaluated by clinician, e.g., massive GI bleed, ICH).
* **Criteria 3**: Severe organ involvement (Liver: AST/ALT ≥1000; CNS: impaired consciousness/encephalopathy; Heart: myocarditis).

## 6. Acute Exacerbation Management & Resuscitation

### Acute Management of Dengue & Severe Dengue
* **Mild/Febrile Phase**: PO Paracetamol ONLY. Strict avoidance of NSAIDs, Aspirin, or IM injections (high risk of catastrophic bleeding). PO Rehydration.
* **Severe Dengue / Warning Signs (Critical Phase 24-48 hrs)**:
  * Strict I/O monitoring, hourly vitals, serial Hematocrit checks.
  * Resuscitation: Isotonic crystalloids (0.9% NaCl or Hartmann's). Start at 5-7 ml/kg/hr, titrate to response (Urine output ≥0.5 ml/kg/hr, stabilizing Hct).
  * DSS: Fluid boluses (10-20 ml/kg over 15-30 mins). If unresponsive, switch to Colloids (e.g., Dextran, Albumin). Blood transfusion ONLY if significant overt bleeding or ↑ pulse rate with rapidly ↓ Hct.
* **Recovery Phase**: STOP IV fluids immediately to prevent iatrogenic pulmonary edema as vascular permeability normalizes and fluids auto-reabsorb.

### Acute Management of Scrub Typhus
* **First Line**: PO/IV Doxycycline (rapid defervescence within 48h is diagnostic).
* **Alternatives**: PO Azithromycin (preferred in pregnancy, children <8 years, or known Doxy resistance).

### Acute Management of Melioidosis (Intensive Phase)
* **Duration**: Minimum 10-14 days IV (extend to 4-8 weeks for deep-seated abscesses, osteomyelitis, CNS infection).
* **First Line**: IV Ceftazidime OR IV Meropenem (if critically ill/septic shock).
* **Adjuncts**: Surgical drainage of large macroscopic abscesses (prostate, liver).

### Acute Management of Leptospirosis (Weil's Disease)
* **Mild**: PO Doxycycline or PO Amoxicillin.
* **Severe (Weil's)**: IV Penicillin G or IV Ceftriaxone.
* **Jarisch-Herxheimer Reaction**: Monitor for acute worsening of fever/shock within hours of starting antibiotics due to massive spirochete lysis.
* **Supportive**: Prompt hemodialysis for AKI; mechanical ventilation for pulmonary alveolar hemorrhage.

### Acute Management of Enteric Fever (Typhoid)
* **Empiric Rx (High resistance in Asia)**: IV Ceftriaxone OR PO Azithromycin.
* **Susceptible Strains**: PO Ciprofloxacin. (Note: High rates of Extensively Drug-Resistant (XDR) Salmonella in Pakistan/India limits use of fluoroquinolones).
* **Severe Typhoid (Shock/Delirium)**: Add IV Dexamethasone (proven mortality benefit).

### Acute Management of Rabies Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP)
* **Wound Care**: Immediate, rigorous flushing with soap and water/povidone-iodine for ≥15 minutes (most critical step). DO NOT suture immediately.
* **Passive Immunization**: Rabies Immunoglobulin (RIG). Must infiltrate as much as possible *into and around* the wound itself. Give on Day 0.
* **Active Immunization**: Rabies cell-culture vaccine (IM deltoid). Regimen: Days 0, 3, 7, 14.

## 7. Chronic Management Algorithm & Disease-Modifying Therapies

### Melioidosis Eradication Phase
* **Goal**: Prevent relapse (relapse rate high without eradication).
* **Regimen**: PO Co-trimoxazole (TMP-SMX).
* **Duration**: 3-6 months. Folic acid supplementation required to prevent marrow suppression. Alternative: PO Amoxicillin-Clavulanate (if TMP-SMX allergic/intolerant).

### Typhoid Chronic Carrier State Management
* **Definition**: Excretion of S. Typhi in stool for >12 months (often asymptomatic, localized to gallbladder).
* **Pharmacotherapy**: Prolonged course (4-6 weeks) of PO Ciprofloxacin or Amoxicillin.
* **Surgical Step-Up**: Cholecystectomy if gallstones present (stones act as biofilm nidus for bacilli) and medical therapy fails.

### Pre-Travel Counseling & Vaccinations (HK Context)
* **General Vector Control**: DEET (≥20-30%) or Picaridin repellents, Permethrin-treated clothing, bed nets.
* **Yellow Fever (黃熱病)**: Live-attenuated vaccine. Required for entry to endemic zones (Africa/South America). Contraindicated in severe immunocompromise, egg allergy, age <6 mos.
* **Typhoid Vaccine**: PO live-attenuated (Ty21a) OR IM Vi capsular polysaccharide. Efficacy ~50-70%.
* **Japanese Encephalitis Vaccine**: Recommended for >1 month rural stay in endemic regions in Asia (pigs/rice paddies).
* **Rabies Pre-Exposure Prophylaxis (PrEP)**: Recommended for high-risk travelers (spelunkers, long-term rural travelers). Given on Days 0, 7, 21/28. Eliminates need for RIG if bitten later (only requires 2 booster doses).

## 8. Risk Stratification, Clinical Scoring Systems & Prognosis

### Dengue WHO 2009 Classification System
* **Dengue without warning signs**: Fever + 2 criteria (nausea, rash, aches, tourniquet +ve, leukopenia). Prognosis: Self-limiting.
* **Dengue with warning signs**: Abdo pain, persistent vomiting, clinical fluid accumulation, mucosal bleed, lethargy, liver >2cm, ↑ Hct with concurrent rapid ↓ Platelets. Prognosis: High risk for severe dengue, mandates admission.
* **Severe Dengue**: Severe plasma leak (Shock/Resp distress), Severe bleeding, Severe organ involvement (AST>1000, impaired sensorium). Prognosis: High mortality without protocolized fluid resuscitation.

### Melioidosis Risk Stratification
* **High-Risk Hosts**: Diabetes Mellitus (most significant risk factor, RR >10), chronic renal failure, thalassemia, excess alcohol use.
* **Prognosis**: Septicemic form mortality >40% despite antibiotics. High risk of latency and relapse.

### Typhoid Complication Timeline Risk
* **Week 1**: Bacteremia (low risk of immediate death).
* **Week 2**: Organomegaly (moderate risk).
* **Week 3-4**: Highest risk for surgical emergencies (intestinal perforation, severe GI bleeding) and typhoid encephalopathy.

## 9. Multi-System Complications

### Complications of Dengue
* **Cardiovascular**: Dengue Shock Syndrome (DSS), myocardial depression/myocarditis.
* **Hematological**: Massive GI/intracranial hemorrhage, DIC.
* **Hepatic**: Acute Liver Failure (ALF) - classically AST > ALT.
* **Neurological**: Post-infectious encephalomyelitis, Guillain-Barré Syndrome.

### Complications of Scrub Typhus
* **Pulmonary**: ARDS (frequent cause of death).
* **Neurological**: Meningoencephalitis, cranial nerve palsies.
* **Renal**: Acute Kidney Injury (ATN secondary to shock).

### Complications of Leptospirosis (Weil's Disease)
* **Pulmonary**: Pulmonary Alveolar Hemorrhage (PAH) - sudden onset massive hemoptysis, very high mortality.
* **Renal**: Hypokalemic non-oliguric AKI early → oliguric Acute Tubular Necrosis late.
* **Hepatic**: Severe hyperbilirubinemia, acalculous cholecystitis.

### Complications of Zika Infection
* **Neurological**: Guillain-Barré Syndrome (GBS) in adults.
* **Fetal/Congenital Zika Syndrome**: Vertical transmission → Severe microcephaly, cerebral calcifications, macular scarring, clubfoot.

### Complications of Enteric Fever (Typhoid)
* **Gastrointestinal**: Terminal ileum necrosis → Intestinal perforation → Fecal peritonitis.
* **Hepatobiliary**: Chronic carrier state in gallbladder, acute cholecystitis.
* **Neurological**: Typhoid encephalopathy (mutism, psychosis).
* **Musculoskeletal**: Osteomyelitis (especially in patients with Sickle Cell Disease).

## 10. Examiner's Pearls

* Viva Question: What is Faget's sign? Answer: Relative bradycardia in the face of high fever.
* Trap: Assuming a fast heart rate rules out typhoid. Pearl: Faget's sign is classic for Typhoid, Legionella, Leptospirosis, and Yellow Fever, but is often absent in critically ill or hypotensive patients.
* Pitfall: Giving NSAIDs for "bone-break fever" in Dengue. Never use NSAIDs; always use Paracetamol to avoid triggering catastrophic hemorrhage.
* Viva Question: Which tropical pathogen causes pneumonia associated with prostatic and splenic abscesses? Answer: Burkholderia pseudomallei (Melioidosis).
* Trap: Discharging a Dengue patient because their fever breaks. Pearl: The defervescence phase (days 3-7) is the most critical period when massive capillary leak and DSS occur; rigorous monitoring must continue.
* Pitfall: Missing the eschar in a patient with fever and rash after a hike. You must strictly examine hidden areas like the groin, axillae, perineum, and inframammary folds for Scrub Typhus.
* Viva Question: What is the classic laboratory pattern of jaundice in Leptospirosis? Answer: Severe hyperbilirubinemia with surprisingly mild elevations in AST/ALT, differentiating it from severe acute viral hepatitis.
* Trap: Stopping Melioidosis treatment after 2 weeks of IV antibiotics when the patient feels better. Pearl: You must strictly transition to a 3-6 month oral eradication phase (TMP-SMX) to prevent fatal relapse.
* Pitfall: Rehydrating a recovering Severe Dengue patient at the same rapid rate. Stop IV fluids promptly during the recovery phase to prevent iatrogenic pulmonary edema as extravascular fluid re-enters the circulation.
* Viva Question: A patient presents with fever, deep jaundice, conjunctival suffusion without exudate, and calf tenderness. Diagnosis? Answer: Weil's Disease (severe Leptospirosis).
