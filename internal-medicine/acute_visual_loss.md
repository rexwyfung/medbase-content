# Acute Visual Loss (急性視力喪失)

## 1. Clinical Definition, Etiologies & Local Epidemiology

### Clinical Definition of Acute Visual Loss
* **Acute Visual Loss (急性視力喪失)**: Rapid deterioration in visual acuity (VA) or visual field (VF) over seconds, minutes, or days.
* **Transient Monocular Visual Loss (Amaurosis Fugax / 暫時性黑矇)**: Complete/partial visual loss resolving spontaneously within 24 hrs (usually <10 mins); heralds impending stroke/permanent occlusion.

### Etiologies of Painless Acute Visual Loss
* **Retinal Artery Occlusion (CRAO/BRAO)**: Embolic (carotid/cardiac) or thrombotic.
* **Retinal Vein Occlusion (CRVO/BRVO)**: Venous stasis/thrombosis secondary to HTN, DM, hyperviscosity.
* **Vitreous Haemorrhage (玻璃體出血)**: Proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), retinal tear, posterior vitreous detachment (PVD).
* **Retinal Detachment (視網膜剝離)**: Rhegmatogenous (retinal tear), tractional (DM), exudative.
* **Non-Arteritic Anterior Ischaemic Optic Neuropathy (NA-AION)**: Microvascular infarction of optic nerve head.

### Etiologies of Painful Acute Visual Loss
* **Acute Angle-Closure Crisis (AACC / 急性閉角型青光眼)**: Blockage of aqueous humor drainage.
* **Optic Neuritis (視神經炎)**: Demyelination, strongly associated with Multiple Sclerosis (MS) or Neuromyelitis Optica (NMO).
* **Arteritic Anterior Ischaemic Optic Neuropathy (A-AION)**: Secondary to Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA / 巨細胞動脈炎).
* **Endophthalmitis (眼內炎)**: Severe intraocular infection (post-op or endogenous).

### Hong Kong Local Epidemiology for Acute Visual Loss
* **AACC**: High prevalence in older Chinese females due to anatomically shallow anterior chambers (short axial length).
* **NA-AION vs A-AION**: NA-AION highly prevalent due to high rates of DM/HTN in HK; A-AION/GCA is relatively rare in Asian populations but cannot be missed.
* **Diabetic Eye Disease**: Leading cause of working-age blindness in HK; prominent cause of sudden visual loss via vitreous haemorrhage/tractional RD.

## 2. Systemic Pathophysiology & Molecular Mechanisms

### Pathophysiology of Retinal Vascular Occlusions
* **CRAO**: Occlusion of central retinal artery (first branch of ophthalmic artery) → acute ischaemia of inner retina. Macula may be spared if supplied by cilioretinal artery (~20% of pop).
* **CRVO**: Compression of central retinal vein by atherosclerotic central retinal artery at lamina cribrosa → turbulent flow → thrombosis → ↑ venous pressure → retinal ischaemia, macular edema, VEGF release.

### Pathophysiology of Retinal Detachment & Vitreous Haemorrhage
* **Rhegmatogenous RD**: Liquefied vitreous enters subretinal space through full-thickness retinal break → separates neurosensory retina from Retinal Pigment Epithelium (RPE).
* **Vitreous Haemorrhage**: Ischaemia (e.g., severe DM) → VEGF production → neovascularization. Fragile new vessels tear easily (often during PVD) → bleed into vitreous cavity.

### Pathophysiology of Optic Neuropathies
* **Optic Neuritis**: Autoimmune demyelination of optic nerve (Type IV hypersensitivity) → conduction block.
* **A-AION (GCA)**: Granulomatous inflammation of large/medium vessels (temporal/ophthalmic arteries) → intimal hyperplasia → luminal occlusion → optic nerve head infarction.
* **NA-AION**: Transient hypoperfusion of short posterior ciliary arteries (often nocturnal hypotension + vascular risk factors) → localized compartment syndrome in crowded optic disc ("disc at risk").

### Pathophysiology of Acute Angle-Closure Crisis
* **Pupillary Block**: Resistance to aqueous flow from posterior to anterior chamber at pupil margin → pressure builds in posterior chamber → peripheral iris bowed forward → physical occlusion of trabecular meshwork → rapid ↑ Intraocular Pressure (IOP).

## 3. Clinical Assessment (Detailed History & System-Specific Physical Signs ONLY)

### History Taking for Acute Visual Loss
* **Onset & Duration**: Seconds (CRAO/Amaurosis Fugax), hours-days (Optic Neuritis, RD).
* **Pain Characteristics**: 
  * Severe pain, N/V, halo around lights → **AACC**.
  * Pain exacerbated by eye movement → **Optic Neuritis**.
  * Scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, proximal muscle ache (PMR) → **GCA/A-AION**.
* **Visual Symptoms**:
  * "Curtain falling" over vision → **Amaurosis Fugax, RD**.
  * Flashes (Photopsia) & Floaters → **PVD, Retinal Tear, RD**.
  * Red desaturation (loss of color vision) → **Optic Neuritis**.
* **Systemic History**: CV risk factors, recent intraocular surgery (Endophthalmitis), autoimmune disease.

### Physical Signs of Retinal Artery & Vein Occlusion
* **CRAO Signs**:
  * +ve Relative Afferent Pupillary Defect (RAPD / 相對性瞳孔傳入障礙).
  * Pale/opaque retina.
  * **Cherry-red spot (櫻桃紅斑)** at macula (choroidal circulation visible through thin fovea).
  * "Box-carring" (fragmentation of blood columns) in arterioles.
* **CRVO Signs**:
  * "Blood and thunder" fundus: diffuse retinal haemorrhages (flame-shaped & dot-blot).
  * Dilated, tortuous veins.
  * Cotton wool spots (nerve fiber layer infarctions).

### Physical Signs of Retinal Detachment & Vitreous Haemorrhage
* **Retinal Detachment**: Loss of red reflex, corrugated/grey elevated retina, Shafer's sign (tobacco dust/pigment cells in anterior vitreous).
* **Vitreous Haemorrhage**: Diminished/absent red reflex, obscured fundal view, visible blood in vitreous.

### Physical Signs of Acute Angle-Closure Crisis (AACC)
* **Anterior Segment**: Ciliary flush (circumcorneal injection), hazy/cloudy cornea (edema), shallow anterior chamber.
* **Pupil**: Mid-dilated, fixed, vertically oval pupil.
* **Palpation**: Globe feels "rock hard".

### Physical Signs of Optic Neuropathies
* **Optic Neuritis**: +ve RAPD, ↓ color vision (dyschromatopsia), Central scotoma. Fundus often normal ("patient sees nothing, doctor sees nothing") unless papillitis present (1/3).
* **A-AION**: +ve RAPD, pale/swollen optic disc (often chalky white), tender/nodular/pulseless temporal artery.
* **NA-AION**: +ve RAPD, swollen/hyperemic optic disc with splinter haemorrhages, altitudinal visual field defect.

## 4. Differential Diagnoses & Discriminatory Features

### Discriminatory Features: Painful vs Painless Acute Visual Loss
| Condition | Pain Profile | Pupil (RAPD) | Fundoscopy / Anterior Segment Findings |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **AACC** | Severe, + N/V, halos | Mid-dilated, fixed | Hazy cornea, shallow AC, rock-hard eye |
| **Optic Neuritis** | Pain on eye movement | +ve RAPD | Normal disc (2/3) or swollen (1/3), central scotoma |
| **GCA (A-AION)** | Headache, jaw claudication | +ve RAPD | Pale/chalky swollen disc, altitudinal field defect |
| **Endophthalmitis**| Severe, post-op/systemic infection | Normal / sluggish | Hypopyon, severe vitritis, absent red reflex |
| **CRAO** | Painless | +ve RAPD | Pale retina, cherry-red spot |
| **CRVO** | Painless | None / mild RAPD | Extensive haemorrhages, cotton wool spots |
| **Retinal Detachment**| Painless, flashes/floaters | None / mild RAPD | Elevated grey retina |
| **Vitreous Haemorrhage**| Painless, sudden floaters/blur | None | Obscured fundus view, no red reflex |

### Discriminatory Features: Pattern of Visual Field Defect
| Field Defect | Localizing Significance | Typical Etiology |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Central Scotoma** | Macula or Optic Nerve | Optic Neuritis, Macular disease |
| **Altitudinal Defect** | Optic Nerve Head (superior/inferior half) | NA-AION, A-AION |
| **Peripheral loss expanding centrally** | Peripheral Retina | Retinal Detachment |

## 5. Tiered Investigations (Bedside, Bloods, Imaging, Biopsy & Diagnostic Criteria)

### Bedside Investigations for Acute Visual Loss
* **Visual Acuity (VA)**: Snellen chart / Pinhole testing (corrects refractive errors).
* **Tonometry**: Measures IOP (Normal: 10-21 mmHg; AACC often >40-60 mmHg).
* **Slit-Lamp Examination**: Assesses anterior chamber depth, cells/flare (uveitis/endophthalmitis).
* **Direct/Indirect Fundoscopy**: Essential for retinal and optic disc evaluation.

### Blood Tests for Acute Visual Loss
* **ESR & CRP**: Urgent stat tests for ANY older patient with visual loss (ruling out GCA).
* **Vascular Screen**: Fasting glucose, HbA1c, Lipid profile (for CRAO, CRVO, NA-AION).
* **Hypercoagulability Screen**: For young patients with CRVO/CRAO (Protein C/S, Antithrombin III, Factor V Leiden, APLA).

### Imaging for Acute Visual Loss
* **Ocular Coherence Tomography (OCT)**: Diagnoses macular edema (CRVO), subtle retinal detachments, optic nerve head swelling.
* **B-scan Ultrasonography**: Essential if fundus view obscured (e.g., dense cataract, vitreous haemorrhage) to rule out Retinal Detachment or mass.
* **Fundus Fluorescein Angiography (FFA)**: Differentiates ischaemic vs non-ischaemic CRVO; shows delayed arterial filling in CRAO.
* **Carotid Doppler Ultrasound**: Mandatory for CRAO/Amaurosis Fugax to detect internal carotid artery stenosis.
* **MRI Brain and Orbits (with Gadolinium)**: Mandatory for Optic Neuritis (assesses demyelinating plaques/MS risk) and atypical visual loss (chiasmal lesions).

### Special Tests and Diagnostic Criteria for Giant Cell Arteritis
* **Temporal Artery Biopsy (TAB)**: Gold standard. Reveals multinucleated giant cells, destruction of internal elastic lamina, intimal thickening. (Do NOT delay steroids waiting for biopsy).
* **ACR 1990 Criteria for GCA** (≥3 required): Age >50, New localized headache, Temporal artery abnormality (tender/pulseless), ESR >50 mm/hr, Abnormal artery biopsy.

## 6. Acute Exacerbation Management & Resuscitation (Flares/Emergencies)

### Acute Management of Acute Angle-Closure Crisis (AACC)
* **Goal**: Immediate ↓ IOP to save optic nerve. Time-critical ophthalmic emergency.
* **Topical Medical Rx**: 
  * Timolol 0.5% (Beta-blocker) → ↓ aqueous production.
  * Apraclonidine 1% (Alpha-2 agonist) → ↓ aqueous production.
  * Pilocarpine 2-4% (Cholinergic) → pupillary constriction (pulls iris away from trabecular meshwork; only effective once IOP <40 mmHg).
* **Systemic Medical Rx**: 
  * IV/PO Acetazolamide 500mg (Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor) → ↓ aqueous production.
  * IV Mannitol (hyperosmotic agent) if IOP refractory.
* **Analgesia/Antiemetics**: IM/PO to control severe pain and vomiting.

### Acute Management of Arteritic AION (Giant Cell Arteritis)
* **Immediate Intervention**: High-dose systemic steroids to prevent fellow eye involvement (50% risk within days).
* **Regimen**: IV Methylprednisolone 1g daily for 3 days → step down to PO Prednisolone 1 mg/kg/day.
* **Referral**: Urgent HA ophthalmology and rheumatology referral; book TAB within 1-2 weeks.

### Acute Management of Retinal Artery Occlusion (CRAO)
* **Goal**: Dislodge embolus (controversial efficacy, window <4-6 hrs).
* **Resuscitation Maneuvers**:
  * Ocular massage (cycles of firm pressure and release).
  * Anterior chamber paracentesis (draw out 0.1-0.2 mL aqueous).
  * Medical IOP lowering (Acetazolamide, Timolol) to ↑ perfusion gradient.
* **Stroke Protocol**: Treat as acute stroke equivalent (urgent neurology evaluation, MRI brain, carotid Doppler, ECG/Echo).

### Acute Management of Optic Neuritis
* **Steroid Therapy**: IV Methylprednisolone 1g daily for 3 days, followed by PO taper (ONTT trial protocol). ↑ rate of visual recovery, but does not alter final VA outcome.
* **Contraindication**: PO Prednisolone ALONE increases recurrence risk and is strictly contraindicated.

### Acute Management of Endophthalmitis
* **Immediate Rx**: Intravitreal antibiotics (Vancomycin + Ceftazidime) via pars plana injection.
* **Surgical Rx**: Pars plana vitrectomy (PPV) for severe cases (visual acuity of light perception only).

## 7. Chronic Management Algorithm & Disease-Modifying Therapies (Pharmacology & Lifestyle)

### Chronic Management of Retinal Vein Occlusion (CRVO/BRVO)
* **Macular Edema Rx**: Intravitreal anti-VEGF injections (e.g., Ranibizumab, Aflibercept) or Intravitreal steroid implants (Ozurdex).
* **Ischaemic Complications**: Panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) laser if neovascularization occurs (prevents neovascular glaucoma/vitreous haemorrhage).

### Chronic Management of Retinal Detachment & Vitreous Haemorrhage
* **Rhegmatogenous RD**: 
  * Pars Plana Vitrectomy (PPV) with gas/silicone oil tamponade.
  * Scleral buckling (silicone band compresses sclera inwards).
  * Pneumatic retinopexy (gas bubble injection + laser).
* **Vitreous Haemorrhage**: Observe for clearing (head elevation, avoid heavy lifting). If non-clearing (>1-3 months) or +ve RD → PPV. 

### Chronic Management of Acute Angle-Closure Crisis
* **Definitive Rx**: Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI) - creates hole in iris to bypass pupillary block.
* **Prophylaxis**: LPI MUST be performed on the fellow eye, as risk of AACC is ~50% within 5 years.

### Chronic Management of Vascular Ocular Conditions (CRAO, NA-AION, Amaurosis Fugax)
* **Secondary Prevention**: Antiplatelet therapy (Aspirin/Clopidogrel).
* **Vascular Optimization**: Statin therapy, aggressive BP control (avoid nocturnal hypotension in NA-AION), tight glycemic control (HbA1c <7%).
* **Surgical**: Carotid endarterectomy (CEA) if symptomatic stenosis >70% in Amaurosis Fugax/CRAO.

## 8. Risk Stratification, Clinical Scoring Systems & Prognosis

### Prognosis of Retinal Artery vs Vein Occlusion
* **CRAO Prognosis**: Extremely poor. 90% have final VA ≤ counting fingers.
* **CRVO Prognosis**: Depends on perfusion status. Ischaemic CRVO (<5% maintain reading vision); Non-ischaemic (50% recover functional vision).

### Risk Stratification of Optic Neuritis (Multiple Sclerosis Risk)
* **Brain MRI Prognostication**:
  * 0 white matter lesions: ~25% risk of MS at 15 years.
  * ≥1 white matter lesion: ~72% risk of MS at 15 years.

### Prognostic Factors for Retinal Detachment
* **Macula-On RD**: Good visual prognosis if urgently repaired (<24-48 hrs).
* **Macula-Off RD**: Poor visual prognosis; requires repair within 1-2 weeks, but central vision rarely returns to normal.

## 9. Multi-System Complications (End-Organ Damage & Drug Adverse Effects)

### Ocular Complications of Acute Visual Loss
* **Neovascular Glaucoma (NVG)**: "90-day glaucoma". Severe complication of ischaemic CRVO/CRAO where VEGF drives iris neovascularization (rubeosis iridis), closing the drainage angle. Highly refractory.
* **Proliferative Vitreoretinopathy (PVR)**: Scar tissue formation on retina post-RD; leading cause of RD surgery failure.

### Systemic Complications associated with Acute Visual Loss
* **Amaurosis Fugax / CRAO**: High short-term risk of catastrophic cerebral infarction (stroke).
* **Giant Cell Arteritis**: Aortic aneurysm/dissection, myocardial infarction, stroke, permanent bilateral blindness.

### Drug Adverse Effects in Ocular Management
* **Acetazolamide (Systemic)**: Hypokalemia, metabolic acidosis, paresthesia, Stevens-Johnson Syndrome (sulfa allergy).
* **Topical Beta-Blockers (Timolol)**: Systemic absorption → bronchospasm (fatal in severe asthma/COPD), bradycardia.
* **Intravitreal Anti-VEGF/Steroids**: Endophthalmitis, retinal detachment, steroid-induced cataract/glaucoma.

## 10. Examiner's Pearls

* Viva Question: Patient presents with painless sudden visual loss, pale retina, and cherry-red spot. What is the most critical immediate systemic investigation? Answer: Carotid Doppler ultrasound and urgent stroke workup to prevent cerebral infarction.
* Trap: Prescribing oral prednisolone alone for an acute episode of Optic Neuritis. Pearl: Oral prednisolone monotherapy increases the recurrence rate of optic neuritis; must use IV methylprednisolone followed by oral taper, or observe without steroids.
* Pitfall: Delaying systemic steroid therapy in suspected Giant Cell Arteritis while waiting for a temporal artery biopsy. Steroids must be started immediately to save the contralateral eye, and biopsy remains valid for up to 1-2 weeks post-steroid initiation.
* Viva Question: Why do patients with Acute Angle-Closure Crisis present with nausea and vomiting? Answer: Massive sudden elevation of intraocular pressure stimulates the vagus nerve (oculocardiac reflex), triggering severe systemic autonomic symptoms.
* Trap: Waiting for full visual field defect before suspecting retinal detachment in a patient with new flashes and floaters. Pearl: Flashes and floaters indicate posterior vitreous detachment or a retinal tear; immediate dilated fundoscopy by an ophthalmologist is required to prevent progression to macula-off retinal detachment.
* Pitfall: Missing an RAPD in a patient with dense cataracts. Cataracts do NOT cause an RAPD; if an RAPD is present, there is co-existing optic nerve or extensive retinal pathology.
