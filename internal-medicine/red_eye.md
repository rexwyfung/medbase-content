# The Red Eye (紅眼症)

## 1. Clinical Definition, Etiologies & Local Epidemiology

### Clinical Definition of Red Eye
* **Red Eye (紅眼症)**: A cardinal sign of ocular inflammation characterized by hyperaemia (engorgement) of the superficial conjunctival, episcleral, or deep ciliary blood vessels. 
* Represents a spectrum ranging from benign, self-limiting conditions to sight-threatening ophthalmological emergencies.

### Anatomical Etiologies of Red Eye
* **Conjunctival**: Viral Conjunctivitis (結膜炎), Bacterial Conjunctivitis, Allergic Conjunctivitis, Subconjunctival Haemorrhage (結膜下出血).
* **Corneal**: Infectious Keratitis (角膜炎) [Bacterial, HSV, Fungal, Acanthamoeba], Corneal Abrasion (角膜擦傷), Corneal Foreign Body.
* **Uveal/Anterior Chamber**: Anterior Uveitis (前葡萄膜炎) [Iritis/Iridocyclitis], Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma (急性閉角型青光眼).
* **Scleral/Episcleral**: Episcleritis (表層鞏膜炎), Scleritis (鞏膜炎).

### Local Epidemiology of Red Eye in Hong Kong
* **Viral Conjunctivitis**: Highly endemic in HK; outbreaks peak in summer (adenovirus/enterovirus) at schools/camps.
* **Microbial Keratitis**: High incidence associated with high myopia rates and widespread cosmetic/soft contact lens (CL) usage in young adults. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* is the leading CL-related pathogen.
* **Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma (AACG)**: Notably high prevalence in elderly Southern Chinese females due to anatomically shallower anterior chambers and shorter axial lengths compared to Caucasians.
* **Autoimmune Ocular Disease**: High prevalence of HLA-B27 in the local population correlates with seronegative spondyloarthropathy-associated Anterior Uveitis.

## 2. Systemic Pathophysiology & Molecular Mechanisms

### Pathophysiology of Conjunctivitis
* **Viral/Bacterial**: Pathogen invasion triggers localized immune response → release of histamine, prostaglandins, leukotrienes → vasodilation of superficial conjunctival vessels (chemosis and hyperaemia) + increased goblet cell secretion (exudate/discharge).
* **Allergic**: IgE-mediated Type I hypersensitivity reaction to airborne allergens (e.g., dust mites, pollen) → mast cell degranulation → intense pruritus and papillary reaction.

### Pathophysiology of Keratitis and Corneal Abrasions
* **Bacterial**: Epithelial defect (e.g., from CL microtrauma or foreign body) permits bacterial entry into stroma → matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and bacterial toxins destroy collagen matrix → inflammatory cell infiltration (stromal infiltrate) → hypopyon formation → potential rapid corneal melt and perforation.
* **HSV**: Reactivation of dormant Herpes Simplex Virus Type 1 from the trigeminal ganglion (V1) → travels via ophthalmic nerve to cornea → active viral replication in corneal epithelium → cell death leading to classic branching (dendritic) ulceration.

### Pathophysiology of Anterior Uveitis
* **Autoimmune mechanism**: Breakdown of the blood-aqueous barrier → leakage of proteins (flare) and leukocytes (cells) into the anterior chamber. Often immune-complex mediated or HLA-B27 associated (molecular mimicry with gut/GU pathogens e.g., *Klebsiella*, *Chlamydia*).
* **Sequelae**: Fibrinous exudate causes adhesion between the iris and anterior lens capsule (posterior synechiae) → fixed, irregular pupil.

### Pathophysiology of Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma (AACG)
* **Pupillary Block (Classic)**: Mid-dilated pupil (e.g., in dim light, stress, or anticholinergic use) maximizes iris-lens contact → aqueous humour trapped in posterior chamber → pressure pushes peripheral iris forward (iris bombé) → mechanical occlusion of the trabecular meshwork in the anterior chamber angle → rapid, severe ↑ intraocular pressure (IOP).

### Pathophysiology of Scleritis vs Episcleritis
* **Episcleritis**: Benign, localized inflammation of the superficial episcleral vascular plexus; largely idiopathic.
* **Scleritis**: Deep, destructive granulomatous inflammation of the scleral collagen, typically representing a localized manifestation of systemic vasculitis or connective tissue disease (e.g., Rheumatoid Arthritis, GPA) via Type III immune complex deposition.

## 3. Clinical Assessment (Detailed History & System-Specific Physical Signs ONLY)

### Red Flag Symptoms of Red Eye (Require Urgent Ophthalmology Referral)
* Severe, deep eye pain (e.g., waking patient at night).
* Photophobia (light sensitivity indicating corneal or uveal involvement).
* Blurred or ↓ visual acuity.
* Haloes around lights (indicates corneal oedema from high IOP).
* Unilateral presentation with systemic symptoms (nausea, vomiting, headache).

### History Taking for Red Eye
* **Onset & Laterality**: Sequential bilateral (viral), simultaneous bilateral (allergic), strictly unilateral (keratitis, AACG, uveitis).
* **Pain Characteristics**: Gritty/foreign body sensation (conjunctivitis/abrasion), severe boring pain (scleritis), photophobia/aching (uveitis/keratitis).
* **Visual Symptoms**: Visual loss, refractive shifts, floaters.
* **Discharge**: Purulent/crusted in AM (bacterial), watery (viral/allergic), none (uveitis, glaucoma).
* **Predisposing Factors**: Contact lens hygiene (sleeping/swimming in lenses), recent trauma/vegetative matter, recent upper respiratory tract infection (URTI) or sick contacts.
* **Systemic Review**: Joint pain, lower back pain, skin rashes, oral/genital ulcers, bowel symptoms (suggests Autoimmune Uveitis/Scleritis).

### Physical Examination of the Anterior Segment (Signs)
* **Hyperaemia Pattern**: 
  * Conjunctival injection: diffuse, prominent at fornices (Conjunctivitis).
  * Ciliary flush (Circumcorneal injection): intense red ring immediately around the limbus (Keratitis, Uveitis, AACG).
  * Sectoral engorgement: localized wedge of redness (Episcleritis).
* **Pupil Abnormalities**:
  * Fixed, mid-dilated pupil (AACG).
  * Constricted (miotic), irregular pupil (Anterior Uveitis due to spasm/synechiae).
* **Corneal Appearance**:
  * Hazy/steamy (Corneal oedema in AACG).
  * White opacity (Corneal ulcer/infiltrate in Keratitis).
* **Subconjunctival Haemorrhage**: Sharply demarcated, dense red patch hiding underlying sclera, normal vision, painless.

## 4. Differential Diagnoses & Discriminatory Features

### Differentiating Dangers of the Red Eye (Diagnostic Matrix)
| Feature | Conjunctivitis | Keratitis (Ulcer) | Anterior Uveitis | Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pain** | Gritty / Mild | Severe + FB sensation | Moderate ache + Photophobia | Severe + Headache / Nausea |
| **Vision** | Normal | Decreased | Mildly Decreased | Markedly Decreased (Haloes) |
| **Discharge** | Watery / Purulent | Watery / Purulent | None | None |
| **Pupil Size** | Normal | Normal or Small | Small (Miotic) / Irregular | Fixed, Mid-dilated |
| **Hyperaemia** | Diffuse | Ciliary flush | Ciliary flush | Ciliary flush |
| **Cornea** | Clear | Opacity / Infiltrate | Clear (usually) | Steamy / Hazy (Oedema) |

### Differentiating Episcleritis vs Scleritis
| Feature | Episcleritis | Scleritis |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Pain** | Mild / Discomfort | Severe, boring, radiates to face, disrupts sleep |
| **Vessels** | Superficial, mobile over sclera | Deep, immobile, criss-crossing |
| **Vision Loss**| None | Potential |
| **Systemic Dz** | Rare | High association (RA, Autoimmune) |

## 5. Tiered Investigations (Bedside, Bloods, Imaging, Biopsy & Diagnostic Criteria)

### Bedside Ophthalmic Investigations for Red Eye
* **Visual Acuity (VA)**: Snellen chart (essential first step). ↓ VA indicates involvement of visual axis (cornea/anterior chamber).
* **Tonometry (IOP Measurement)**: Applanation or Tono-Pen. 
  * Normal: 10-21 mmHg. 
  * AACG: often >50 mmHg.
* **Fluorescein Staining**: 
  * Instill sterile saline/local anaesthetic moistened strip into lower conjunctival fornix, ask patient to blink to distribute, examine with Cobalt Blue or Wood's lamp.
  * **Corneal Abrasion**: Brilliant green epithelial defect. Record size, shape, and location.
  * **HSV Keratitis**: Branching (dendritic) ulcer with terminal bulbs.
* **Seidel Test**: Application of fluorescein to assess for aqueous leak indicating full-thickness corneal perforation. +ve if a stream of clear fluid dilutes the green dye (waterfall effect).

### Slit Lamp Examination Findings
* **Anterior Chamber**: 
  * Cells (WBCs) and Flare (protein) → diagnostic for Anterior Uveitis.
  * Hypopyon (leukocyte settling inferiorly) → severe keratitis, severe uveitis, or endophthalmitis.
* **Cornea**: Depth of infiltrate, keratic precipitates (WBCs on corneal endothelium seen in uveitis).
* **Phenylephrine Test**: Topical 10% phenylephrine drops blanch superficial episcleral vessels (Episcleritis) but do NOT blanch deep scleral vessels (Scleritis).

### Laboratory Investigations for Complex Red Eye
* **Microbiology**: 
  * **Corneal Scrape**: Gram stain + Culture (Blood agar, Chocolate agar, Sabouraud for fungi) mandatory for severe microbial keratitis.
  * **Conjunctival Swab**: PCR for Adenovirus, HSV, *Chlamydia trachomatis*, *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* (in neonates/hyperpurulent cases).
* **Immunological / Rheumatology Panel** (indicated for recurrent/bilateral non-granulomatous Anterior Uveitis or Scleritis):
  * HLA-B27, ANA, RF, Anti-CCP, ANCA, ACE level (Sarcoidosis), Syphilis serology (VDRL/TPPA), CXR (TB/Sarcoid).

## 6. Acute Exacerbation Management & Resuscitation (Flares/Emergencies)

### Acute Management of Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma (Ophthalmic Emergency)
* **Goal**: Rapid reduction of IOP to save the optic nerve.
* **Medical Therapy (Sequential / Concurrent)**:
  * Systemic: IV/PO Acetazolamide (Carbonic anhydrase inhibitor) 500mg stat (avoid if sulfa allergy).
  * Topical Beta-blocker: Timolol 0.5% drops stat.
  * Topical Alpha-2 agonist: Apraclonidine 1% or Brimonidine 0.2% stat.
  * Topical Miotic: Pilocarpine 2% or 4% drops (applied 1-2 hours AFTER pressure starts dropping to break pupillary block; ineffective if IOP >40 mmHg due to ischemic iris sphincter).
  * Hyperosmotic agent: IV Mannitol 20% or PO Glycerol (if IOP highly refractory).
* **Definitive Treatment**: Urgent Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI) once cornea clears.

### Acute Management of Microbial Keratitis (Corneal Ulcer)
* **Goal**: Eradicate pathogen, prevent corneal melt.
* **Immediate Actions**: STOP contact lens wear. Discard lenses and cases. Do NOT patch the eye.
* **Pharmacotherapy (Post-scrape)**: 
  * Intensive topical antibiotics: e.g., Levofloxacin 1.5% drops hourly day and night OR fortified dual therapy (Cefazolin 5% + Gentamicin/Tobramycin 1.5% hourly).
  * Cycloplegics: Cyclopentolate 1% TDS for pain relief (relieves ciliary spasm).
  * NO TOPICAL STEROIDS acutely until infection is controlled and under strict ophthalmology supervision.

### Acute Management of Anterior Uveitis
* **Topical Corticosteroids**: Prednisolone acetate 1% drops (frequent instillation, e.g., 1-2 hourly) to suppress intraocular inflammation.
* **Cycloplegics/Mydriatics**: Cyclopentolate 1% or Atropine 1% drops to break and prevent posterior synechiae formation and relieve pain from ciliary spasm.

### Acute Management of Corneal Abrasion / Foreign Body
* **Removal**: Foreign body removal at slit lamp under topical anaesthesia (e.g., Tetracaine) using a sterile needle or cotton bud.
* **Prophylaxis**: Topical broad-spectrum antibiotic (Chloramphenicol or Ofloxacin ointment QDS for 5-7 days).
* **Pain Control**: Oral analgesics. (Never discharge a patient with topical anaesthetic drops due to risk of toxic keratopathy/corneal melt).

## 7. Chronic Management Algorithm & Disease-Modifying Therapies (Pharmacology & Lifestyle)

### Chronic Management of Primary Angle-Closure Glaucoma Disease
* **Prophylaxis**: Prophylactic YAG Laser Peripheral Iridotomy (LPI) for the contralateral eye is mandatory.
* **Long-term Medical Rx**: Stepwise addition to maintain target IOP:
  1. Prostaglandin analogues (Latanoprost) - increases uveoscleral outflow.
  2. Beta-blockers (Timolol) - decreases aqueous production.
  3. Topical Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors (Dorzolamide).
* **Surgical Options**: Phacoemulsification (cataract extraction opens the angle) or Trabeculectomy if medically refractory.

### Chronic Management of Recurrent Autoimmune Uveitis & Scleritis
* **Systemic Immunosuppression**: Managed jointly with Rheumatology.
* **Step-up Therapy**: 
  1. Oral NSAIDs (for mild recurrent scleritis/uveitis).
  2. Systemic Corticosteroids (Prednisolone) for acute severe bridging.
  3. Conventional DMARDs (Methotrexate, Mycophenolate Mofetil, Azathioprine).
  4. Biologic Agents (TNF-alpha inhibitors e.g., Adalimumab, Infliximab) for refractory HLA-B27/RA associated ocular disease.

### Preventive Lifestyle and Hygiene Modifications
* **Contact Lens Hygiene**: DO NOT wash lenses with tap water (Acanthamoeba risk). Avoid sleeping or swimming in lenses. Replace cases monthly. Use daily disposables if possible.
* **Infection Control**: Strict hand hygiene, do not share towels, stay home from school/work for 1-2 weeks if diagnosed with epidemic keratoconjunctivitis (Adenovirus).
* **UV Protection**: Wraparound UV-blocking sunglasses to prevent pterygium progression and cataracts.

## 8. Risk Stratification, Clinical Scoring Systems & Prognosis

### Risk Stratification for Microbial Keratitis (Severity Criteria)
* **High-Risk Features (High likelihood of permanent visual loss)**:
  * Size > 2 mm.
  * Central visual axis involvement.
  * Deep stromal tissue involvement or thinning.
  * Significant anterior chamber reaction (Hypopyon).
  * Treatment: Requires admission, daily ophthalmology review, and fortified hourly antibiotics.

### Prognostic Indicators in Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma
* **Duration of IOP Elevation**: IOP remaining >40-50 mmHg for > 24-48 hours causes irreversible ischemic damage to the optic nerve head (glaucomatous optic atrophy).
* **Bilateral Risk**: The contralateral unaffected eye has a 50% risk of developing an AACG attack within 5 years if prophylactic LPI is not performed.

### Systemic Association Risk in Scleritis and Uveitis
* **Scleritis**: >50% of patients have an underlying systemic autoimmune disease (most commonly RA, followed by GPA/Wegener's).
* **Necrotizing Scleritis**: Carries a high mortality risk (poor prognosis) if untreated, as it usually reflects severe systemic necrotizing vasculitis.

## 9. Multi-System Complications (End-Organ Damage & Drug Adverse Effects)

### Ocular and Systemic Complications of Untreated Red Eye
* **Keratitis**: Corneal scarring (opacity), corneal perforation, endophthalmitis (loss of the eye).
* **Anterior Uveitis**: Posterior synechiae, secondary band keratopathy, secondary cataract, secondary inflammatory glaucoma.
* **Scleritis**: Scleral thinning (scleromalacia perforans), globe rupture.
* **AACG**: Permanent blindness (optic neuropathy), glaukomflecken (lens epithelial necrosis).

### Adverse Effects of Ophthalmic Medications
* **Topical Corticosteroids (e.g., Prednisolone, Dexamethasone)**: 
  * Steroid-induced secondary open-angle glaucoma (steroid responders).
  * Premature posterior subcapsular cataract formation.
  * Exacerbation of undiagnosed HSV keratitis leading to massive geographic ulcer and corneal melt.
* **Topical Beta-Blockers (e.g., Timolol)**: 
  * Systemic absorption via nasolacrimal duct → bronchospasm (fatal in asthma/COPD), bradycardia, heart block.
* **Topical Cycloplegics (e.g., Cyclopentolate, Atropine)**:
  * Anticholinergic systemic toxicity (delirium, tachycardia, flushing) especially in children/elderly.
* **Topical Prostaglandin Analogues (e.g., Latanoprost)**:
  * Iris hyperpigmentation, eyelash lengthening (hypertrichosis), periorbital fat atrophy.

## 10. Examiner's Pearls

* Trap: Prescribing topical corticosteroids for an undiagnosed red eye in the primary care setting. Pearl: Never prescribe topical steroids without a formal slit-lamp examination to definitively exclude HSV keratitis, as steroids trigger massive viral replication, geographic ulceration, and potential corneal perforation.
* Viva Question: What is the Seidel test? Answer: Application of fluorescein to assess for an aqueous leak indicating full-thickness corneal perforation; positive if a stream of clear fluid is seen diluting the green dye under cobalt blue light.
* Pitfall: Discharging a patient with a corneal abrasion and a bottle of topical anaesthetic (e.g., Tetracaine) for pain control. Repeated use of topical anaesthetics blocks epithelial healing and leads to severe toxic keratopathy, corneal melt, and permanent blindness.
* Viva Question: What are the key differentiating signs of scleritis versus episcleritis on physical examination? Answer: Scleritis presents with severe, boring eye pain that wakes the patient at night, and the deeply injected criss-crossing vessels do NOT blanch with the application of topical 10% phenylephrine.
* Pitfall: Missing a Pseudomonas infection in a contact lens wearer presenting with a red eye. Always assume *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* is the causative organism in contact lens-related keratitis and treat aggressively with topical fluoroquinolones or fortified antibiotics; never recommend patching the eye.
* Trap: Focusing solely on the affected eye during an acute angle-closure glaucoma attack. Pearl: The contralateral eye shares the same narrow-angle anatomy and is at extremely high risk; always arrange prophylactic laser peripheral iridotomy (LPI) for the unaffected eye.
* Viva Question: What classical physical sign differentiates anterior uveitis from acute angle-closure glaucoma? Answer: Anterior uveitis typically presents with a constricted (miotic) and irregular pupil due to ciliary spasm and posterior synechiae, whereas AACG classically presents with a fixed, mid-dilated pupil.
